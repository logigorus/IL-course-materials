Phase 1 — Overview of the Topic and Key Empirics

Few questions in comparative political economy are older or more contested than whether democratic or autocratic regimes are better vehicles for development. The question has mattered at every hinge point of the twentieth century — the Soviet industrialization debates, the East Asian “miracle,” the Washington Consensus era, and, now, the rise of a confident authoritarian capitalism centered on China.

What the modern literature has settled is mostly the shape of the empirical facts, not their interpretation. The descriptive statistics presented below are computed from the Varieties of Democracy (V-Dem) Electoral Democracy (“polyarchy”) Index, v15, merged with World Bank World Development Indicators for 173 countries over 1960–2023 (about 11,000 country-year observations).1 A stable set of stylized facts emerges.

Four stylized facts

  1. Democracies grow slightly faster on average. Mean annual GDP-per-capita growth is 2.19% in liberal democracies and 2.21% in electoral democracies, versus 1.97% in electoral autocracies and 1.75% in closed autocracies (author’s calculations from V-Dem v15 and World Bank WDI; see Figure 1). The headline democracy–growth premium is real but modest — on the order of half a percentage point per year, broadly consistent with the meta-analytic estimate in Doucouliagos and Ulubasoglu (2008).
  2. Autocracies are dramatically more volatile. The standard deviation of annual growth is 8.16% in closed autocracies and 6.37% in electoral autocracies, versus 5.31% in electoral democracies and 3.79% in liberal democracies. This volatility asymmetry … Przeworski et al. (2000) call it the central regime-type result of their dataset.
  3. The human-development gradient is steep and one-directional. Mean life expectancy rises from 58 years in closed autocracies to 75 in liberal democracies; under-five mortality falls from 115 per thousand to 16 per thousand — a roughly seven-fold gap (cf. Besley and Kudamatsu 2006; Kudamatsu 2012).
  4. “Autocratic growth miracles” are concentrated in a handful of cases. Of the top fifteen fastest-growing countries (years of >7% per-capita growth), eleven had mean polyarchy scores below 0.5. China alone accounts for 36 such episodes — more than the next two combined.

These facts do not adjudicate the question; they only show why it remains live. Mean growth favors democracies but by a narrow margin. Human development favors democracies robustly, but almost every poor liberal democracy is also rich by global standards — so the correlation is confounded by income. And the extreme autocratic successes (post-1978 China, post-1965 Singapore, post-1961 South Korea) are real and sustained, even if rare.

Their headline estimate is that a country that transitions from nondemocracy to democracy achieves about 20% higher GDP per capita over the next 25 years (Acemoglu et al. 2019, 47–48).

4. Przeworski, Alvarez, Cheibub, and Limongi. 2000. Democracy and Development.

Using a dichotomous regime measure for 135 countries (excluding major oil producers) over 1950–1990, Przeworski and colleagues test whether regime type causes growth. Their well-known empirical regularity (originally in Przeworski and Limongi 1997) is that no democracy has ever fallen above roughly $6,055 GDP per capita (1985 PPP). This is best characterized as evidence for an “exogenous” theory of democratic survival rather than for the “endogenous” theory that wealth causes democratization (which Przeworski and Limongi explicitly reject).

Factual error caught Original draft said “141 countries.” The actual sample, after excluding major oil producers, is 135 countries. Caught and corrected after a web-search check against the Cambridge UP page and secondary sources. Capability since: web search · May 2025
Conceptual error caught Original draft labelled the $6,055 finding their “endogenous-democratization threshold.” That is backwards: Przeworski & Limongi reject the endogenous theory and offer this finding as evidence for the exogenous theory of democratic survival. Rewrote the passage and added the missing P&L 1997 reference. Capability since: domain-knowledge synthesis · 2024
