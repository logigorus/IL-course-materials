Week 1: Contesting Globalization
DWA 103 — The Contested World Economy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Presentation Overview
• Course Text: Eric Helleiner, The Contested World Economy: The Deep and Global Roots of International Political Economy (Cambridge University Press, 2023).
• Key Argument: The intellectual history of International Political Economy (IPE) is far deeper and more globally diverse than standard textbooks suggest. Pre-1945 debates were not exclusive to Western thinkers nor limited to the traditional "trilateral" debate between liberals, neomercantilists, and Marxists.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
I. What is International Political Economy?
Definition & Origins
• Focus: IPE investigates the intersection of politics and economics at the global level, focusing on the interactions between states, markets, and societies.
• The 1970s Rebirth: The formalization of the field was sparked by three major global shifts:
    1. The end of dollar-gold convertibility in 1971.
    2. The OPEC oil crisis of 1973.
    3. Demands for a New International Economic Order (NIEO) in 1974.
• The "Rebirth" Thesis: Scholar Susan Strange argued that IPE was not a new "birth" but a revival of pre-1945 political economy that had been temporarily marginalized.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
II. The Book's Central Claims
Claim 1: A More Global History
• Thinkers from Asia, Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East were significant contributors to economic thought, not just passive recipients.
• Non-European thinkers innovatively adapted European ideas or developed independent versions of economic theory.
• Intellectual influence flowed in multiple directions, challenging the "West to the rest" narrative.
Claim 2: Beyond the "Three Orthodoxies"
• Standard history focuses on Economic Liberalism, Neomercantilism, and Marxism.
• Helleiner expands this to include Autarkism, Environmentalism, Feminism, and Pan-movements (Pan-Africanism, Pan-Islamism, Pan-Asianism), as well as unique visions of economic regionalism.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
III. The Three Orthodoxies — Elaborated
A. Economic Liberalism
• Core Pillars: Free trade, free markets, and the belief that commerce fosters global peace and individual liberty.
• Key Figures: Adam Smith, David Ricardo, Richard Cobden, and John Stuart Mill.
• Smith’s Insight: The "simple system of natural liberty" is driven by individuals pursuing self-interest (e.g., the butcher and brewer) rather than government direction,.
• Global Adaptations:
    ◦ Americas: Carlos Calvo (Argentina), José da Silva Lisboa (Brazil).
    ◦ Asia: Taguchi Ukichi (Japan), Yan Fu (China), Rammohun Roy (India).
    ◦ Africa/Ottoman: Olaudah Equiano, Hassuna D'Ghies.
    ◦ Note: Some Chinese scholars argued Confucian texts endorsed market policies long before Smith.
B. Neomercantilism
• Core Pillars: Strategic protectionism and government activism to build state wealth and industrial power.
• Key Figures: Alexander Hamilton, Friedrich List, Henry Carey, and Sergei Witte.
• Key Concept: "Productive Power": List argued that the capacity to produce wealth is more important than wealth itself; industrialization is the foundation of national strength.
• The "Ladder" Metaphor: List famously critiqued Britain for "kicking away the ladder" of protectionism once they reached the summit of industrial greatness.
• Global Reach: Indigenous neomercantilism existed in Japan before Western contact, and was later championed by figures like Sun Yat-sen (China) and Mahadev Govind Ranade (India).
C. Marxism
• Core Pillars: Class conflict, global exploitation, and the necessity of a revolutionary overthrow of capitalism.
• Theories of Imperialism: Lenin viewed imperialism as the "highest stage" of capitalism driven by finance capital, while Luxemburg saw it as a necessary expansion into non-capitalist territories.
• Global Diffusion: Marxist thought was adapted globally by figures such as Mao Zedong (China), M.N. Roy (India), and José Carlos Mariátegui (Peru).
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
IV. Beyond the Orthodoxies: Hidden Perspectives
• Autarkism: Advocated for total national economic self-sufficiency, moving beyond mere protectionism.
• Environmentalism: Pre-1945 thinkers offered early critiques of the ecological degradation caused by industrial capitalism.
• Feminism: Early feminist economists highlighted the patriarchal structure of the global economy and women’s subordinate roles.
• Pan-Africanism: Figures like W.E.B. Du Bois and Marcus Garvey sought economic solidarity to challenge the subordinate position of the African diaspora.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
V. Why This History Matters
• Contemporary Relevance: Current debates on gender, race, and the environment are rooted in these overlooked historical discussions.
• Global Conversations: The goal is to build a more inclusive field by acknowledging diverse intellectual traditions.
• Correcting "Path Dependence": Challenging the narrow focus established by the 1970s "foundational moment" of IPE.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VI. Key Concepts Glossary
Term
Definition
IPE
The study of the intersection of politics and economics at the global level.
Economic Liberalism
Advocates for free trade and markets to promote prosperity and peace.
Neomercantilism
Uses strategic protectionism to build state power and industry.
Productive Power
A nation’s broad economic capacity and ability to produce wealth.
Infant Industry
Temporary protection for new industries to allow them to mature.
Autarky
A policy of national economic self-sufficiency.
