Week 1: Contesting Globalization
DWA 103 — The Contested World Economy
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Presentation Overview
• Course Text: Eric Helleiner, The Contested World Economy: The Deep and Global Roots of International Political Economy (Cambridge University Press, 2023).
• Key Argument: The intellectual history of International Political Economy (IPE) is far deeper and more globally diverse than standard textbooks suggest. Pre-1945 debates were not exclusive to Western thinkers nor limited to the traditional "trilateral" debate between liberals, neomercantilists, and Marxists.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
I. What is International Political Economy?
Definition & Origins
• Focus: IPE investigates the intersection of politics and economics at the global level, focusing on the interactions between states, markets, and societies.
• The 1970s Rebirth: The formalization of the field was sparked by three major global shifts:
    1. The end of dollar-gold convertibility in 1971.
    2. The OPEC oil crisis of 1973.
    3. Demands for a New International Economic Order (NIEO) in 1974.
• The "Rebirth" Thesis: Scholar Susan Strange argued that IPE was not a new "birth" but a revival of pre-1945 political economy that had been temporarily marginalized.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
II. The Book's Central Claims
Claim 1: A More Global History
• Thinkers from Asia, Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East were significant contributors to economic thought, not just passive recipients.
• Non-European thinkers innovatively adapted European ideas or developed independent versions of economic theory.
• Intellectual influence flowed in multiple directions, challenging the "West to the rest" narrative.
Claim 2: Beyond the "Three Orthodoxies"
• Standard history focuses on Economic Liberalism, Neomercantilism, and Marxism.
• Helleiner expands this to include Autarkism, Environmentalism, Feminism, and Pan-movements (Pan-Africanism, Pan-Islamism, Pan-Asianism), as well as unique visions of economic regionalism.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
III. The Three Orthodoxies — Elaborated
A. Economic Liberalism
• Core Pillars: Free trade, free markets, and the belief that commerce fosters global peace and individual liberty.
• Key Figures: Adam Smith, David Ricardo, Richard Cobden, and John Stuart Mill.
• Smith's Insight: The "simple system of natural liberty" is driven by individuals pursuing self-interest (e.g., the butcher and brewer) rather than government direction,.
• Global Adaptations:
    ◦ Americas: Carlos Calvo (Argentina), José da Silva Lisboa (Brazil).
    ◦ Asia: Taguchi Ukichi (Japan), Yan Fu (China), Rammohun Roy (India).
    ◦ Africa/Ottoman: Olaudah Equiano, Hassuna D'Ghies.
    ◦ Note: Some Chinese scholars argued Confucian texts endorsed market policies long before Smith.
B. Neomercantilism
• Core Pillars: Strategic protectionism and government activism to build state wealth and industrial power.
• Key Figures: Alexander Hamilton, Friedrich List, Henry Carey, and Sergei Witte.
• Key Concept: "Productive Power": List argued that the capacity to produce wealth is more important than wealth itself; industrialization is the foundation of national strength.
• The "Ladder" Metaphor: List famously critiqued Britain for "kicking away the ladder" of protectionism once they reached the summit of industrial greatness.
• Global Reach: Indigenous neomercantilism existed in Japan before Western contact, and was later championed by figures like Sun Yat-sen (China) and Mahadev Govind Ranade (India).
C. Marxism
• Core Pillars: Class conflict, global exploitation, and the necessity of a revolutionary overthrow of capitalism.
• Theories of Imperialism: Lenin viewed imperialism as the "highest stage" of capitalism driven by finance capital, while Luxemburg saw it as a necessary expansion into non-capitalist territories.
• Global Diffusion: Marxist thought was adapted globally by figures such as Mao Zedong (China), M.N. Roy (India), and José Carlos Mariátegui (Peru).
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
IV. Beyond the Orthodoxies: Hidden Perspectives
• Autarkism: Advocated for total national economic self-sufficiency, moving beyond mere protectionism.
• Environmentalism: Pre-1945 thinkers offered early critiques of the ecological degradation caused by industrial capitalism.
• Feminism: Early feminist economists highlighted the patriarchal structure of the global economy and women's subordinate roles.
• Pan-Africanism: Figures like W.E.B. Du Bois and Marcus Garvey sought economic solidarity to challenge the subordinate position of the African diaspora.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
V. Why This History Matters
• Contemporary Relevance: Current debates on gender, race, and the environment are rooted in these overlooked historical discussions.
• Global Conversations: The goal is to build a more inclusive field by acknowledging diverse intellectual traditions.
• Correcting "Path Dependence": Challenging the narrow focus established by the 1970s "foundational moment" of IPE.

VI. "The Power of the Powerless": Carney at Davos
Context: A Rupture, Not a Transition
• Setting: PM Mark Carney addressed the World Economic Forum in Davos amid escalating U.S. tariff threats, territorial pressures on Greenland, and a global reassessment of the liberal international order.
• Central Metaphor: Carney invoked Václav Havel's 1978 essay "The Power of the Powerless," describing a greengrocer who places a sign reading "Workers of the world, unite!" in his window—not because he believes it, but to signal compliance and avoid trouble.
• The Analogy: For decades, middle powers like Canada "placed the sign in the window" by participating in the rituals of the "rules-based international order" while knowing the system was "partially false"—that the strongest exempted themselves when convenient and trade rules were enforced asymmetrically.
• The Declaration: "Friends, it is time for companies and countries to take their signs down... We are in the midst of a rupture, not a transition."

Key Quotes for Discussion
• "The strong do what they can, and the weak suffer what they must." — Carney invokes Thucydides to describe the return of power politics.
• "You cannot 'live within the lie' of mutual benefit through integration when integration becomes the source of your subordination."
• "A country that cannot feed itself, fuel itself, or defend itself has few options."
• "Nostalgia is not a strategy."
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
VII. Mapping Carney to the Orthodoxies
How the Speech Reflects IPE's Contested Traditions

| Course Framework | Carney's Application |
|------------------|----------------------|
| **Economic Liberalism** | Carney critiques its "fiction": free trade provided public goods (open sea lanes, stable finance, collective security), but the strongest always exempted themselves. The rules were "enforced asymmetrically." |
| **Neomercantilism** | The speech is deeply neomercantilist: "building strength at home," strategic protectionism, doubling defense spending, fast-tracking investment in energy, AI, and critical minerals. Echoes List's "productive power." |
| **Autarkism** | Carney warns that if middle powers retreat to "a world of fortresses," everyone will be "poorer, more fragile, and less sustainable"—but acknowledges the impulse is "understandable." |
| **Beyond the Orthodoxies** | Carney's "values-based realism" represents a hybrid: principled commitment to sovereignty, human rights, and territorial integrity, but pragmatic about diverging interests and incremental progress. |

The "Ladder" Returns
• Just as List accused Britain of "kicking away the ladder," Carney implies the U.S. is using "economic integration as weapons"—tariffs as leverage, financial infrastructure as coercion, supply chains as vulnerabilities.
• Middle powers must now build their own ladders through diversification, strategic partnerships, and collective investments in resilience.

The Productive Power Parallel
• Carney's emphasis on Canada's domestic capacity mirrors List's concept: "Canada has what the world wants. We are an energy superpower. We hold vast reserves of critical minerals. We have the most educated population in the world."
• The goal is not wealth alone, but the *capacity to produce* that wealth—and the sovereignty that capacity confers.

VIII. Discussion: Theory Meets Practice
Engaging with the Carney Speech

**Question 1: Orthodoxy or Hybrid?**
Which of the "three orthodoxies" (Economic Liberalism, Neomercantilism, Marxism) best describes Carney's worldview? Is "values-based realism" a genuine fourth tradition—or simply neomercantilism with better branding?

**Question 2: Havel's Greengrocer and the Liberal Order**
Carney argues that for decades, middle powers "lived within a lie" by praising a rules-based order they knew was "partially false." 
- Was participating in this "fiction" actually beneficial? For whom?
- What are the risks of "taking the sign down"?
- How does this critique apply to non-state actors (corporations, NGOs, international institutions)?

**Question 3: The Ladder, Redux**
Carney implies the U.S. is now "kicking away the ladder" by weaponizing the very economic integration it promoted. 
- How does this compare to List's 19th-century critique of Britain?
- Is there a meaningful difference between a hegemon abandoning free trade principles and a rising power adopting protectionism?
- What does this suggest about the *durability* of liberal economic norms?

**Question 4: Who Gets to Define "Values"?**
Carney frames Canada's approach as "values-based realism," emphasizing sovereignty, human rights, and territorial integrity. 
- Who decides which values are universal?
- How might thinkers from Africa, Asia, or Latin America (whom Helleiner highlights) respond to this framing?
- Is "values-based realism" genuinely inclusive—or another form of Western-centric ordering?

**Question 5: The Middle Power Dilemma**
Carney argues that middle powers must choose: "compete with each other for favour or combine to create a third path with impact."
- What historical examples can you think of where middle powers successfully coordinated against great power pressure?
- What are the collective action problems that might prevent such coordination?
- How does the rise of China complicate this binary choice?

IX. Key Concepts Glossary
Term | Definition
-----|------------
IPE | The study of the intersection of politics and economics at the global level.
Economic Liberalism | Advocates for free trade and markets to promote prosperity and peace.
Neomercantilism | Uses strategic protectionism to build state power and industry.
Productive Power | A nation's broad economic capacity and ability to produce wealth.
Infant Industry | Temporary protection for new industries to allow them to mature.
Autarky | A policy of national economic self-sufficiency.
**Rules-Based International Order** | The post-WWII system of multilateral institutions, trade agreements, and norms—now under strain.
**Middle Power** | A state that is not a great power but exercises significant influence through diplomacy, coalitions, and institutional engagement.
**Values-Based Realism** | Carney's framework: principled commitment to core values (sovereignty, human rights) combined with pragmatic engagement based on interests.
**Strategic Autonomy** | The capacity of a state to act independently in critical sectors (energy, food, defense) without being vulnerable to external coercion.
