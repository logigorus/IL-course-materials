Overview

The main writing assignment in this course is a 3,000–4,000-word review of the scholarly literature on a question in the study of authoritarianism (the bibliography does not count toward the word limit). You can expand on a topic from the course or choose one of your own, after checking it with me. You will find the scholarly conversation on that question, organize it, and explain what scholars have learned, where they disagree, and what remains unresolved.

This is an upper-division seminar, so the review must do one more thing: make an argument about the state of the field. Which side of the debate has the better evidence? What has the field gotten wrong, or not yet asked? That argument is what you defend, live, on a panel with your classmates.

What a literature review is — and is not

A literature review surveys what scholars have found on a question: where they agree, where they disagree, and why. It then evaluates the state of the field: what is well established, what is contested, what is missing, and what kind of evidence would settle it.

It is not a book report (a summary of source 1, then source 2, then source 3). It is not an op-ed about a regime you dislike. And it is not a research paper testing your own hypothesis. Your own voice comes through in how you organize the field and in how you judge it.

Choosing a topic

Pick one of the ten topics below, or propose your own. A topic of your own is welcome, especially if it lets you go deeper into something we only touched on in class or covers something the course leaves out (for example, transnational repression of exiles and diasporas, gender and autocracy, authoritarian environmentalism and the energy transition, religion and regime legitimacy, personalism and succession, or sanctions against autocracies). Either way, email me your topic before class on Tuesday, November 3: a working question, a sentence on why it matters, and two or three sources you have already found. If you want to talk it through first, come to office hours.

A good topic is narrow enough to read seriously in a few weeks, and broad enough that scholars have actually argued about it. “Russia” is too broad. “Do elections strengthen or weaken electoral autocracies?” is a literature.

⚠️ Starter readings, not required readings.

You do not need to use all 10 readings listed under your topic. Your final bibliography should contain 12–15 sources that you have identified as most relevant to your specific question. Some may come from this list or from the syllabus (including the optional Depth readings); others you will find through your own Google Scholar research. The search process is part of the assignment — follow citation trails, and check recent Annual Reviews for orientation.

Part 1 — Description & Annotated Bibliography

Due Thursday, November 12, 11:59 pm · 10% of the final grade · one PDF or Word file by email

Part 1 stakes out your question, surveys the first 7–9 key sources, and ends with a first statement of what you think the field has right and wrong. It has three components, in this order, in one document.

What counts as a scholarly source?

Prioritize peer-reviewed journal articles in political science, economics, sociology, law and related fields, and books or chapters from university presses. Working papers from recognized research institutions (NBER, V-Dem, the World Bank) are acceptable in moderation. News articles, opinion pieces, think-tank blog posts, Wikipedia and textbooks are not scholarly sources. You may use them, and data sources such as V-Dem or Freedom House, for a fact or a statistic in your description, but they do not count toward your source total.

Essays by scholars in the Journal of Democracy and Foreign Affairs are useful for orientation and do count, but they are not peer-reviewed research: use no more than two in Part 1 and three in the final draft.

Step 1 — Topic description (~600 words)

  1. Define the issue. What research problem are you surveying? Frame it as an area of scholarly debate, not a hypothesis you plan to test.
  2. Establish the context. What are the broad trends? Give the reader a few facts, figures or indicators that show the scope and scale of the issue (V-Dem or Freedom House scores, counts of coups or regime breakdowns, election results, measures of repression or capital flight), with sources.
  3. Define your terms. Authoritarianism research turns on contested concepts (competitive authoritarianism, backsliding, kleptocracy, informational autocracy). Say which definition you are using and whose it is.
  4. Motivate the topic. Why should anyone care? Connect the issue to a current case, a policy debate, or an unresolved scholarly disagreement. Answer the “so what?” question.

Step 2 — Annotated bibliography (7–9 scholarly sources)

For each source:

  1. Full citation in Chicago author-date style, used consistently.
  2. Evidence type (one line). What kind of evidence does the source rest on? For example: cross-national quantitative study, single-country case study, comparative-historical analysis, survey or experiment, formal model, new data set, or an essay synthesizing others’ work.
  3. Summary (2–3 sentences). What is the author’s main argument or finding? Be specific: name the thesis, the evidence or method, and the key result. “This article is about Russia” is not a summary.
  4. Critical engagement (2–3 sentences). How does this source bear on your question? What is most useful about it, and where are its limits (its cases, its period, its data, its definitions)? Note specific pages, figures or quotations you expect to come back to.
One annotated entry, as an example

Guriev, Sergei, and Daniel Treisman. 2019. “Informational Autocrats.” Journal of Economic Perspectives 33 (4): 100–127.

Evidence type: Theoretical argument (a non-technical version of the authors’ formal model), illustrated with cross-national descriptive data and brief cases.

Summary: Guriev and Treisman argue that many contemporary dictators survive by manipulating beliefs about their competence rather than by mass violence. Their ruler co-opts the informed elite and censors selectively in order to look competent, and show that since the 1980s autocracies have become less violent and more likely to hold elections and mimic democratic forms.

Critical engagement: The article gives my review its central distinction (fear versus spin), but its evidence is largely descriptive: the model predicts the trend without testing whether spin causes regime survival. The coding of leaders as “spin” or “fear” dictators is a judgment call worth checking against the regime-level data (see their figures on trends in political killings and elections).

Step 3 — Reflection and working claim (~600 words)

Drawing on the sources you annotated, write a short reflective essay that addresses:

  • Themes. What patterns, points of agreement, or recurring findings appear across your sources? Are there identifiable camps or schools of thought?
  • Gaps and limitations. Where is the research thin, outdated, or narrow (one region, one regime type, one period, one kind of data)? What remains unanswered?
  • Research questions. Pose 2–3 research questions a future project could pursue. You are not committing to answer them; the point is to show that reading the literature turned up real, answerable questions.
  • Working claim. End with one paragraph stating your argument about the field, as it stands now. For example: “The case that elections stabilize autocracies rests mostly on single-party regimes before 2000; it is much weaker for personalist regimes since then.” You are allowed to change your mind later. This paragraph is the first draft of your panel statement.
Finding sources efficiently

Start from the starter readings or a recent review article (the Annual Review of Political Science is the best place to look), then follow citation trails in both directions: what a good article cites, and what cites it. In Google Scholar, “Cited by” does the second.

Weigh recency and influence. A field’s seminal works are the ones everyone cites; its frontier is in the last five years. You need both.

Use a reference manager. Zotero is free and will format your Chicago citations for you.

What you get back: written comments on your question, your sources and your working claim, as soon as I can. This is the stage where a topic that is too broad, too narrow, or short on scholarship gets fixed.

Part 2 — Panel Defense

Tuesday, November 17; Thursday, November 19; Tuesday, November 24 · 10% of the final grade · in class · panels and running order in the shared doc

You will defend your literature review on a panel of four or five students working on related questions. Two panels meet on November 17, two on November 19, and the last on November 24, before our closing discussion. This is a draft-stage defense: you are not expected to have a finished paper. You are expected to know your literature, to have an argument about it, and to answer questions without reading from your notes.

Your statement: 4–5 minutes, hard stop

  1. The question and why it matters (one fact or figure).
  2. The field: two or three camps or findings, naming the key authors and what they found.
  3. Your argument about the field: which side has the better evidence, what the field has missed, or what it has gotten wrong.
  4. The weakest point in your own case, stated honestly. Name it before someone else does.

Questions: 8–10 minutes per panel

After every panelist has spoken, the panel takes open questions from me, from the room, and from each other. Come with one question ready for each of your panel-mates, based on their bibliography. Good questions are specific: a source they missed, a case that cuts against their claim, a definition doing too much work.

Format

Up to three slides (optional). Use them for a figure, a table, or a map of the camps in your literature, not for text you read aloud. Time yourself beforehand: I will stop you at five minutes, wherever you are. The defense is live and in person: no AI.

How the defense is graded

I grade live, during the session, on a one-page rubric.

DimensionTop tier looks like
Command of the literatureNames authors and findings accurately and specifically; distinguishes stronger from weaker work.
Argument & self-critiqueStates a clear claim about the field within the time limit, and names the weakest point in it candidly.

Part 3 — Final Literature Review

Due Thursday, December 10, 11:59 pm (finals period) · 20% of the final grade · no grace period · one PDF or Word file by email

The final paper is a 3,000–4,000-word essay in narrative form, not counting the bibliography. It should read as a coherent account of a scholarly conversation, with an argument running through it: who said what, where they agree, where they disagree, what remains unresolved, and what you make of it. It is not a list of article summaries. The bibliography should contain 12–15 scholarly sources that you have judged most relevant to your question, including the field’s seminal work and at least three sources published since 2021. Some will come from Part 1; others you will find after the feedback, the workshop and the panel.

Suggested structure

  1. Introduction (~600 words). Define the issue and your key terms, give the reader a reason to care (with data or indicators), state the question that organizes the field, state your argument about it, and preview the structure of the paper.
  2. The scholarly conversation (~2,000–2,600 words). The heart of the paper. Organize the literature thematically, around 3–4 themes, debates or findings, and show how different scholars contribute to each. Use specific evidence (findings, data, page numbers), and tell sources apart: not every study is equally rigorous or equally relevant. Include at least one passage on evidence and measurement: how does this field know what it claims to know, and how much should we trust it? (Our Week 10 debate over measuring backsliding is the model.)

Common pitfalls

The book-report structure. Article 1, then Article 2, then Article 3. Organize by theme, not by source.

A review with no argument. A fair summary of every side is a good start, not a finished seminar paper. Say what you think the evidence shows.

Case-study tourism. Ten readings about one country do not make a literature on authoritarianism. If your question is about one case, show what the comparative literature says and how the case fits it.

Treating every study as equal. A large cross-national study and a single-case opinion essay are not the same kind of evidence. Say which is which.

A conclusion that only summarizes. The conclusion should judge the field, not repeat the paper.
