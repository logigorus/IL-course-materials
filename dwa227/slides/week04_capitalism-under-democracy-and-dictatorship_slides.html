Argument 1 · Friedman · the market as a check

History suggests only that capitalism is a necessary condition for political freedom. Clearly it is not a sufficient condition. Milton Friedman, Capitalism and Freedom (1962), ch. 1, “The Relation between Economic Freedom and Political Freedom” (excerpt p. 2)

Argument 1 · Friedman · two ways of coordinating

“Fundamentally, there are only two ways of co-ordinating the economic activities of millions”

One

“central direction involving the use of coercion — the technique of the army and of the modern totalitarian state.”

The other

“voluntary co-operation of individuals — the technique of the market place.”

“Exchange can therefore bring about co-ordination without coercion.”

Friedman 1962, ch. 1 (excerpt p. 4).

Argument 1 · Friedman · concentration and dispersion of power

There can be many millionaires in one large economy. But can there be more than one really outstanding leader, one person on whom the energies and enthusiasms of his countrymen are centered? … there seems to be something like a fixed total of political power to be distributed. Consequently, if economic power is joined to political power, concentration seems almost inevitable. On the other hand, if economic power is kept in separate hands from political power, it can serve as a check and a counter to political power. Friedman 1962, ch. 1 (excerpt p. 5)

Argument 1 · Friedman · the cases he lists

“Economic arrangements that are fundamentally capitalist and political arrangements that are not free”

  • Fascist Italy · Fascist Spain · Germany “at various times in the last seventy years”
  • Japan before World Wars I and II · tsarist Russia “in the decades before World War I”
  • “in each, private enterprise was the dominant form of economic organization”
  • “I know of no example in time or place of a society that has been marked by a large measure of political freedom, and that has not also used something comparable to a free market”

Friedman 1962, ch. 1 (excerpt p. 2). Thesis of the chapter: “a society which is socialist cannot also be democratic, in the sense of guaranteeing individual freedom” (excerpt p. 1).

Context · Hobbes · the state of nature

In such condition there is no place for industry, because the fruit thereof is uncertain: and consequently no culture of the earth; no navigation, nor use of the commodities that may be imported by sea; no commodious building; … no arts; no letters; no society; and which is worst of all, continual fear, and danger of violent death; and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short. Thomas Hobbes, Leviathan (1651), ch. XIII, “Of the Naturall Condition of Mankind, as concerning their Felicity, and Misery”

Not assigned this week (assigned: Narrow Corridor 1–2, Friedman ch. 1, Olson 1993, Martínez 2022). Public-domain text; cited by chapter, the standard convention, since pagination varies by edition.

Context · Locke · the ends of government

The great and chief end, therefore, of men’s uniting into commonwealths, and putting themselves under government, is the preservation of their property. John Locke, Second Treatise of Government (1689), §124

Discussion: as you read Olson, which problem is he solving — Hobbes’s (any power sufficient to end the war of all against all) or Locke’s (a power confined to preserving what people already hold)?

Not assigned this week. Locke glosses “property” broadly, as “Lives, Liberties and Estates” (§87, §123). Cited by section, the standard convention for this text.

Argument 2 · Olson · the stationary bandit

Monarchy is the best kind of government because the King is then owner of the country. Like the owner of a house, when the wiring is wrong, he fixes it. A village monarchist in southern Italy, in Edward Banfield, The Moral Basis of a Backward Society (1958), p. 26 — quoted in Mancur Olson, “Dictatorship, Democracy, and Development,” APSR 87(3), 1993: 567

Argument 2 · Olson · “the first blessing of the invisible hand”

the rational, self-interested leader of a band of roving bandits is led, as though by an invisible hand, to settle down, wear a crown, and replace anarchy with government. Olson 1993: 568. Example on the same page: the warlord Feng Yu-hsiang, 1920s China, “noted for the exceptional extent to which he used his army for suppressing bandits” and for defeating the roving bandit White Wolf.

Diagram: schematic of Olson 1993: 567–569 (“encompassing interest,” p. 569). Not data.

Argument 2 · Olson · “the grasping hand”

The revenue-maximizing tax rate

Tax receipts will — if we start with low taxation — increase as tax rates increase, but after the revenue-maximizing rate is reached, higher tax rates distort incentives and reduce income so much that tax collections fall. The rational self-interested autocrat chooses the revenue-maximizing tax rate. Olson 1993: 569–570

Schematic of Olson 1993: 569–570 (citing Schumpeter and Ibn Khaldun); not data. Worked example on p. 570: with a revenue-maximizing rate of one-third and a majority earning one-third of national income in the market, the autocrat “just breaks even on this last dollar of tax collection,” while a majority at the same rate “would lose two dollars.”

Argument 2 · Olson · “long live the king”

  • “At the limit, when an autocrat has no reason to consider the future output of the society at all, his incentives are those of a roving bandit and that is what he becomes.” (571)
  • “the promises of an autocrat are never completely credible” (571)
  • “There is no private property without government!” (572)
  • “History provides not even a single example of a long and uninterrupted sequence of absolute rulers who continuously respected the property and contract-enforcement rights of their subjects.” (572)

Olson 1993, page numbers per item.

Argument 2 · Olson · democracy, individual rights, and development

the conditions that are needed to have the individual rights needed for maximum economic development are exactly the same conditions that are needed to have a lasting democracy. Olson 1993: 572

“Though experience shows that relatively poor countries can grow extraordinarily rapidly when they have a strong dictator who happens to have unusually good economic policies, such growth lasts only for the ruling span of one or two dictators.” (572)

Olson 1993. Closing line of the article: “The moral appeal of democracy is now almost universally appreciated, but its economic advantages are scarcely understood” (575).

Argument 2 · Olson · “the improbable transition”

autocracy is prevented and democracy permitted by the accidents of history that leave a balance of power or stalemate — a dispersion of force and resources that makes it impossible for any one leader or group to overpower all of the others. Olson 1993: 573
  • England, 1689: “There were no lasting winners in the English civil wars” — Parliament, an independent judiciary, a Bill of Rights (574)
  • “it was in Britain, not very long after the Glorious Revolution, that the Industrial Revolution began” (574)

Olson 1993, page numbers per item.

Argument 3 · Acemoglu & Robinson · the Shackled Leviathan

Repression and dominance are as much in its DNA as they are in the DNA of the Despotic Leviathan. But the shackles prevent it from rearing its fearsome face. How those shackles emerge, and why only some societies have managed to develop them, is the major theme of our book. Daron Acemoglu & James A. Robinson, The Narrow Corridor (2019), p. 27

Book not in the course folder; quotation as reproduced in a published review (CEI, “Best Books of 2019”). Verify against the printed page before quoting further.

Schematic after Acemoglu & Robinson 2019, chs. 1–2 (book not in the course folder; axis and region labels paraphrased — verify the authors’ figure before quoting). Quoted lines: stateless societies “are quite capable of controlling violence and putting a lid on conflict, though as we’ll see this doesn’t bring much liberty” (p. 11); “Life under the yoke of the state can be nasty, brutish, and short too” (p. 12).

Argument 3 · Acemoglu & Robinson · the Red Queen

it takes all the running you can do, to keep in the same place Lewis Carroll, Through the Looking-Glass (1871), as quoted in Acemoglu & Robinson 2019: 41
  • “capable states matched by capable societies” (66)
  • “Once the Leviathan is shackled, society may choose to give it a long leash and allow it to increase its reach so that the state uses its capacity for things that its citizens want and need” (72)
  • Norms in weak-state places “also create a cage, imposing a different but no less disempowering sort of dominance” (19)
