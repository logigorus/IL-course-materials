A framework for the week · Polanyi

The double movement

Marketizing movement (laissez-faire)

Goal — expand the self-regulating market; disembed the economy from social, political, and cultural institutions
Drivers — capital owners, financial institutions, free-market advocates
Methods — deregulation, free trade, privatization, less state intervention

Protective countermovement

Goal — re-embed the economy in social and legal limits; protect human life, nature, and productive enterprise
Drivers — workers, farmers, civic organizations, state regulators — across the political spectrum
Methods — labor law, environmental protection, social insurance, tariffs, central-bank controls

The trigger — fictitious commodities

“The commodity description of labor, land, and money is entirely fictitious.” — Ch. 6: 75–76

Labor — human life and activity. Unregulated: wage collapse, unsafe work, social decay
Land — nature. Unregulated: resource exhaustion, pollution, ecological collapse
Money — purchasing power and credit. Unregulated: speculation, business failure, unemployment

Schema after Polanyi, The Great Transformation (1944): fictitious commodities, Ch. 6, pp. 75–76 (quoted); the double movement, Ch. 11 — not in the course folder; paraphrase, not quotation.

Level 3 · Society — Equiano, 1789

Olaudah Equiano

  • By his own account, born c. 1745 in Essaka, an Igbo town in present-day Nigeria; kidnapped with his sister at age 11
  • Sold repeatedly; crossed the Atlantic as a child aboard a slave ship
  • Purchased his own freedom in 1766, for £40
  • Published The Interesting Narrative of the Life of Olaudah Equiano in 1789, timed to Parliament’s debate on the slave trade — among the first bestselling books published in English by a Black author, and a founding text of the British abolition movement
  • The birthplace claim is contested: historian Vincent Carretta has found naval and baptismal records suggesting a South Carolina birth — which Carretta reads as evidence of Equiano’s skill as a political writer, not proof the Narrative is false

Equiano, The Interesting Narrative (1789); Oxford Faculty of English, “The Interesting Narrative,” Ten-Minute Book Club (on the Carretta research)

Applier — one of this week’s three, worked

Announcing the April 2 tariffs — a 10% minimum on all countries, 34% on China, 20% on the EU — Trump said that “for decades, our country has been looted, pillaged … and plundered by nations near and far, both friend and foe alike” (Kurtzleben, NPR). Polanyi argues that a self-regulating market “could not exist for any length of time without annihilating the human and natural substance of society,” so society moves to protect itself (The Great Transformation, Ch. 1: 3). Here the protecting is done by a state, and NPR reports that Canada “has said it is prepared to impose its own retaliatory tariffs. So has the European Union.” Is state-against-state retaliation still Polanyi’s self-protection of society, or a different mechanism?

Roles: DWA 227 syllabus, “Course Structure.” Reading applied: Polanyi, The Great Transformation (1944), Ch. 1, p. 3. Article: Danielle Kurtzleben, “Trump unveils sweeping 10% tariff and ‘reciprocal’ tariffs on dozens of nations,” NPR, Apr. 2, 2025. Instructor-composed example.

Thursday · in-class exercise — Spot the Applier

Which one is the Applier?

In your discussion group, 8 minutes: find the one genuine Applier question, and name which rubric line each of the other four fails. Then one group defends its pick.

  1. Levitsky & Way, V-Dem, and Fukuyama describe political backsliding; Frieden describes an economic backlash that never fully resolved. Was Fukuyama's error about markets as much as regimes?
  2. TSMC is building three fabs in Phoenix (~$65B, plus up to $6.6B in CHIPS Act funding) instead of expanding in Taiwan — a real case of O'Neil's regionalization. Is that market logic, or the same self-protective impulse Polanyi describes, applied by a state instead of a society?
  3. The 2025 tariffs prove Polanyi was right about the gold standard.
  4. How does O'Neil's regionalization data compare to the trade patterns Frieden describes for 1870–1914?
  5. Like the gold standard, the 2025 tariffs raised consumer prices — Yale Budget Lab put the cost at $2,700–$3,400 per household a year (NPR).
