What a literature review is, and is not

It is
A survey of what scholars have found on a question: where they agree, where they disagree, and why; then a judgment on the state of the field: what is established, contested, missing, and what evidence would settle it.
It is not
A book report (source 1, then source 2, then source 3); an op-ed about a regime you dislike; a research paper testing your own hypothesis.
Too broad
“Russia”
A literature

Part I · Accountability · three directions

Who can hold a government to account?

Vertical. “Vertical accountability captures the extent to which citizens have the power to hold the government accountable.” Mechanisms: free political parties; free and fair elections, including for the chief executive.

Horizontal. “The power of state institutions to oversee the government by demanding information, questioning officials and punishing improper behavior.” Agents: the legislature, the judiciary, ombudsmen, prosecutors, auditors.

Diagonal. “Actions and mechanisms that citizens, civil society organizations… and an independent media can use to hold the government accountable,” such as social mobilization and investigative journalism.

Definitions quoted from the Varieties of Democracy project codebook, version 16 (2026): Vertical, Horizontal and Diagonal Accountability indices. Diagram: instructor-drawn from those definitions.

Part II · The judiciary · Alexander Hamilton, 1788

“The Executive not only dispenses the honors, but holds the sword of the community. The legislature not only commands the purse, but prescribes the rules by which the duties and rights of every citizen are to be regulated. The judiciary, on the contrary, has no influence over either the sword or the purse… It may truly be said to have neither FORCE nor WILL, but merely judgment; and must ultimately depend upon the aid of the executive arm even for the efficacy of its judgments.”Hamilton, Federalist No. 78 (1788)

Part II · The judiciary · Federalist No. 78

Sword, purse, judgment

The Federalist Papers. 85 newspaper essays, 1787–1788, by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison and John Jay under the pen name “Publius,” urging New York to ratify the new United States Constitution.

No. 78 defends an independent federal judiciary whose judges serve “during good behavior,” that is, for life unless impeached.

Hamilton’s conclusion: “the judiciary, from the nature of its functions, will always be the least dangerous to the political rights of the Constitution.”

Efficacy. A ruling takes effect only if others carry it out: police, ministries, banks, election officials.

Hamilton, Federalist No. 78, “The Judiciary Department” (1788). Icons: instructor-drawn.

Part III · Four kinds of courts · Dichio & Logvinenko 2024, Table 1

Stewards, defenders, progenitors, collaborators

Columns: the pressure the court faces from elected officials and other actors, either pro-democratic or anti-democratic.

Rows: what the court itself does with that pressure.

Stewards act “by coordinating with and overseeing elected branches to maintain the constitutional balance of powers.” In democratic crises, courts can act “as progenitors instigating democratic backsliding, … defenders protecting democratic institutions; and … collaborators enabling backsliding.”

Cases this week: Germany 2023 · Colombia 2010 · United States 2026 · Venezuela 2024.

Dichio & Logvinenko 2024: 6. Backsliding: the step-by-step weakening of democratic institutions by those in power (Bermeo 2016).

Prologue · key findings · the six articles in the readings folder

Six findings

United States, 2020 election cases

Dichio & Logvinenko 2024b · 20 federal rulings, 17 interviews

All 20 rulings went against the Trump campaign’s claims: 10 by Democratic appointees, 10 by Republican appointees. Procedural rules such as standing, mootness and laches carried norms of judicial independence.

“culture and practice within institutions eat documents for lunch” (a state supreme court judge, p. 301)

United States, the Supreme Court

Gardbaum 2024 · comparative design

Life tenure, decentralized review and opposition-run states make United States courts hard to capture, yet the same independence lets the Supreme Court pursue democratic backsliding on its own account.

“we are talking about resistance, or ‘speed bumps,’ and certainly not complete insurance or protection” (p. 353)

Latin America

Gamboa, García-Holgado & Gonzalez-Ocantos 2024 · Colombia, Argentina, Mexico

Narrow, procedural, one-shot rulings against power grabs held; long anti-corruption campaigns (Brazil’s Car Wash, Peru, Ecuador) inflamed conflict and helped outsiders.

“Whatever the reason, democracy lived to fight another day and judges emerged from these battles relatively unscathed.” (p. 372)

Turkey

Esen 2025 · 2002–2023

The secular opposition used high courts against an elected government; the governing party then cited the courts’ “democratic deficit” to justify packing them (2010 referendum) and purging them (after 2016).

“Judicial independence is necessary but not sufficient for democratic consolidation.” (p. 16)

Europe: how judges resist

Šipulová 2025 · interviews in 10 European countries

Judges resist on the bench (rulings, timing, references to European courts) and off it (resignations, protest marches, media). Resistance can also shield judges of a former regime from democratic reform.

“Resistance is not a right, a duty or a virtue of judges.” (p. 19)

The whole issue

Dichio & Logvinenko 2024a · introduction

Courts act as stewards, defenders, progenitors or collaborators; their capacity to resist depends on the political context, and courts alone cannot save a democracy.

“the true cures for democracy’s ailments lie beyond the courthouse walls” (p. 10)

Part III · Collaborators · Ginsburg & Moustafa 2008 (depth)

Why authoritarian rulers keep courts

Russia’s “legal dualism” (Popova 2017). Ordinary commercial and administrative cases are decided by law; cases that matter to the Kremlin are decided by political instruction.

Paraphrase of Tom Ginsburg & Tamir Moustafa, “Introduction: The Functions of Courts in Authoritarian Politics,” in Rule by Law (Cambridge, 2008): 1–22, and Maria Popova, “Putin-Style ‘Rule of Law’,” Daedalus 146, no. 2 (2017). Neither text is in the course folder; verify wording before quoting.

Part I · Accountability · Guillermo O’Donnell, 1998

The original distinction

“Reasonably free and fair elections provide a means of vertical accountability in these countries, along with freedoms of speech, the press, and association, which permit citizens to voice social demands to public officials (elected or not) and to denounce these same officials for wrongful acts that they may commit.”O’Donnell 1998: 112–113
“This kind of accountability depends on the existence of state agencies that are legally empowered—and factually willing and able—to take actions ranging from routine oversight to criminal sanctions or impeachment in relation to possibly unlawful actions or omissions by other agents or agencies of the state.”O’Donnell 1998: 117, on horizontal accountability

Part IV · Brazil · courts and judges

Brazil’s courts: who is who

Supreme Federal Court

11 justices, nominated by the president and confirmed by the Senate; retire at 75. Decides constitutional cases and tries the president, ministers and members of Congress for crimes. Many cases are decided by one of two five-justice panels.

Superior Electoral Court

7 members: 3 Supreme Federal Court justices, 2 from the Superior Court of Justice, 2 lawyers. Runs national elections, counts the electronic vote, and can bar candidates from office.

Sérgio Moro

Federal trial judge in Curitiba, 1996–2018

Presided over the Operation Car Wash corruption trials; convicted Lula in 2017; became Bolsonaro’s justice minister in January 2019; elected senator in 2022.

Alexandre de Moraes

Supreme Federal Court justice since 2017 (appointed by President Michel Temer)

President of the Superior Electoral Court, August 2022–June 2024; judge in charge of the inquiries into false news (2019) and the coup plot (2023–2025).

The presidents. Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva (Workers’ Party): president 2003–2010 and since 2023. Jair Bolsonaro (Liberal Party since 2021): president 2019–2022. Flávio Bolsonaro, his eldest son, senator and 2026 presidential candidate.

Constitution of Brazil (1988), arts. 101–102 and 119; court websites. Moraes’s electoral-court term and inquiry roles: Associated Press and Al Jazeera reporting, 2022–2025.

Part VI · Rome · the late Republic

Rome: 82 to 43, before the Common Era

The Republic. Rome was governed by two annually elected consuls, other elected magistrates, popular assemblies and the Senate (former magistrates, serving for life).

Criminal courts (quaestiones) were standing jury courts for specific crimes, such as extortion by provincial governors. Who sat on the juries was a political fight.

Beard, SPQR: A History of Ancient Rome (2015), ch. 1, “Cicero’s Finest Hour”; Plutarch, Life of Sulla 31–33. All years before the Common Era. Paraphrase; the Beard chapter is not in the course folder.

Part VI · Rome · the trial of Verres, 70

A governor, a jury and a timetable

  • 73–71Gaius Verres governs Sicily; accused of extorting the province
  • 70Sicilian cities hire Cicero to prosecute in the extortion court; the jury is all senators
  • 70Defense plan: delay the trial into 69, when allies of Verres would hold office and preside
  • 70Cicero gives a short opening speech (Against Verres I), then calls witnesses at once; Verres flees into exile before the verdict
  • 70Aurelian Law: juries split among senators, wealthy non-senators (equestrians) and treasury officials

Cicero reports Verres’s boast: three years in Sicily were enough — one year’s profits for himself, one for his advocates and protectors, one for the jury.

Part VI · Rome · Cicero and Catiline, 63

“Cicero’s finest hour”

  • 63Cicero is consul. Lucius Sergius Catiline, defeated for the consulship, is accused of plotting to seize power
  • October 63Senate passes its “final decree” (senatus consultum ultimum): the consuls should see that the state comes to no harm
  • November 63Cicero’s speeches against Catiline; Catiline leaves Rome to join an armed force
  • Dec 5, 63Senate debates five arrested conspirators: Julius Caesar proposes life imprisonment, Cato the Younger death. They are strangled that night, without trial
  • 58A law of Publius Clodius exiles anyone who executed a Roman citizen without trial; Cicero leaves Rome
  • 57Cicero recalled from exile
  • 43Cicero himself is proscribed by Mark Antony, Octavian and Lepidus, and killed
