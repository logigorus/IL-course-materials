Linz & Stepan · the case for revising the tripartite scheme

If we use these studies and the traditional tripartite regime type distinction, it turns out that more than 90 percent of modern nondemocratic regimes would have to share the same typological space—“authoritarian.” Obviously, with so many heterogenous countries sharing the same typological “starting place,” this typology of regime type cannot tell us much about the extremely significant range of variation in possible transition paths and consolidation tasks that we believe in fact exists. Linz & Stepan · post-totalitarianism as a continuum

  • Early — “very close to the totalitarian ideal type but differs from it on at least one key dimension, normally some constraints on the leader”
  • Frozen — “almost all the other control mechanisms of the party-state stay in place for a long period and do not evolve”
  • Mature — “significant change in all the dimensions … except that politically the leading role of the official party is still sacrosanct”

Linz & Stepan 1996: 156. Country-years are the authors’ own examples (Czechoslovakia 1977–1989; Hungary 1982–1988).

Guriev & Treisman · the argument

The model of dictatorship that dominated in the twentieth century was based on fear. … The key to such regimes, we argue, is the manipulation of information. Rather than terrorizing or indoctrinating the population, rulers survive by leading citizens to believe—rationally but incorrectly—that they are competent and public-spirited. Guriev & Treisman, “Informational Autocrats,” Journal of Economic Perspectives 33, no. 4 (2019): 100–101

Named cases (p. 102): Chávez’s Venezuela, Putin’s Russia, Fujimori’s Peru, Mahathir’s Malaysia, Orbán’s Hungary, Correa’s Ecuador; Lee Kuan Yew’s Singapore “a pioneer of the model.”

Pei · the case

An even more worrying aspect of the China puzzle is that since Xi Jinping became paramount leader in 2012, the CCP has reverted to a neo-Stalinist path. Xi has revived one-man rule, escalated political repression to its worst level since Mao Zedong died in 1976, reintroduced ideological indoctrination, and launched an aggressive foreign policy that openly challenges the theory and practice of a liberal, rules-governed international order. Pei, “China: Totalitarianism’s Long Shadow,” Journal of Democracy 32, no. 2 (2021): 6

“The China puzzle—a relatively high level of socioeconomic development coexisting with a persistent dictatorship” (p. 5).

Pei · three propositions

  1. Legacies of totalitarianism blunt and neutralize the democratizing effects of economic modernization.
  2. If the interests entrenched in the Leninist party-state remain in place, economic reform will lose momentum and the CCP regime will grow even more resistant to democratization and hostile to democratic values.
  3. Lack of political reform, including democratization, greatly raises the risks of reversion to neo-Stalinist rule.

Pei 2021: 12, 14, 15 (the propositions’ own wording). Same essay, p. 8: “A Leninist party, with its insistence that it must rule alone and free of competition, is the heart of the problem.”

Development and regime type · two readings

Guriev & Treisman

In highly modern countries, the informed elite is generally too large for manipulation to work, and censoring all private media is costly: democracy is the only option. In undeveloped countries, repression often remains more cost effective. But at intermediate levels of development, both democracy and informational autocracy are possible outcomes. Guriev & Treisman 2019: 117

Pei

Communist dictatorships that go for economic reform without political democratization, like those in China and Vietnam, may achieve swift socioeconomic progress. But the legacy institutions of totalitarianism will allow the democratizing effects of development to be blunted and perhaps even neutralized altogether. Pei 2021: 7–8

Both essays start from Lipset, “Some Social Requisites of Democracy” (1959); Pei 2021: 5; Guriev & Treisman 2019: 102, 117.
