Know · collecting information · V-Dem Digital Society Project, 2000–2025

Government monitoring of political content on social media

What is measured. V-Dem asks country experts: “How comprehensive is the surveillance of political content in social media by the government or its agents?” Answers run from “extremely comprehensive” (the government surveils virtually all content) to “not at all.” A statistical measurement model combines the experts’ answers into a score on a continuous scale where 0 is roughly the global average and most values fall between −4 and +3.

How the chart is built. V-Dem codes low numbers as more surveillance; the chart flips the sign so that higher means more monitoring. Each line is the simple average across countries in that regime group in that year (Regimes of the World: closed and electoral autocracies vs. electoral and liberal democracies). Every country counts equally. In 2025 there were 92 autocracies and 87 democracies.

What the numbers show. The autocracy average rises from 0.26 in 2000 to 0.65 in 2010 and 1.04 in 2025: about half the increase before 2010, half after. The democracy average also rises, from −1.42 to −0.88. The gap between the two groups ranges from 1.68 to 2.09 points (1.68 in 2000, 1.92 in 2025). 2025 scores (flipped): North Korea 3.59, China 3.32, Russia 2.75, Myanmar 2.71, Iran 1.92, United States 0.11.

Caveats. Group membership changes year to year, so the lines mix change within countries with countries switching groups. Each estimate has an uncertainty band (not shown); year-to-year moves of a few hundredths are within it. Units include territories V-Dem codes separately (e.g., Hong Kong, Somaliland).

V-Dem Country-Year Dataset v16 (Coppedge et al. 2026, doi:10.23696/vdemds26), variable v2smgovsmmon, model point estimates × −1; regime groups from v2x_regime. Averages computed from the dataset file in the official vdemdata repository.

Know · collecting information · Atlantic Council spyware dataset, 2025

Commercial spyware vendors, by headquarters country

What is measured. The Atlantic Council’s “Mythical Beasts” project maps the companies and people behind commercial spyware: tools sold to governments to break into phones and computers, as Pegasus does. The 2025 update covers 561 entities in 46 countries from 1992 to 2024, including vendors (the firms that build and sell the tools), their subsidiaries, partners, suppliers, holding companies, investors, and key individuals.

What the numbers show. The dataset lists 53 vendors. Israel and Italy are each home to 10, India to 6, the United States to 5, the United Kingdom and the Czech Republic to 3 each, and Luxembourg and Spain to 2 each. Twelve other countries host one vendor each. The money behind the market is concentrated too: U.S.-based investors in spyware firms number 31 (up from 11 in the 2024 edition), followed by Israel (26) and Italy (12).

Caveats. The vendor-by-country counts (and the 53-vendor total) are tallied from the project’s public dataset file, not printed in the report; the dataset’s entity, country, and U.S.-investor totals match the report’s (561, 46, 31). The dataset includes only firms identifiable from public records and investigations. Headquarters country is where a firm is registered, not where its customers are; Citizen Lab found Pegasus infections in 45 countries.

Atlantic Council, “Mythical Beasts: Diving into the Depths of the Global Spyware Market,” Sep 10, 2025; dataset github.com/ac-csi/mythical-beasts (nodes file), tallied Oct 2026. Citizen Lab, “Hide and Seek,” 2018.

Influence behaviour · negative incentives · Access Now, 2025

Internet shutdowns in 2025, by trigger

What is measured. Access Now defines a shutdown as “an intentional disruption of internet or electronic communications, rendering them inaccessible or effectively unusable, for a specific population or within a location, often to exert control over the flow of information.” The count includes nationwide and regional blackouts, mobile-only cuts, throttling, and blocks of specific platforms. Each shutdown is coded by the situation in which it was imposed.

What the numbers show. Of 313 shutdowns in 52 countries, 125 (40%) occurred during armed conflict, the leading trigger for the third year in a row. Protests and political instability account for 64, and “information control” (Access Now’s label) for 48. Elections (12) and school exams (11) are smaller categories.

Platform blocks, a subset. Blocks of specific platforms reached 94 in 40 countries in 2025, up from 31 in 2016 and 77 in 2024. The most-blocked platforms were Facebook (31), WhatsApp (27), Telegram (23), Instagram (20), and X (20).

Caveats. Triggers sum to 319, not 313, because some shutdowns have more than one. Counts depend on what can be detected and reported; short or local shutdowns in closed countries are more likely to be missed.

Access Now, Rising Repression Meets Global Resistance: Internet Shutdowns in 2025, March 2026: totals p. 5; triggers chart p. 7; platform blocks p. 21. Definition: Access Now’s standard #KeepItOn definition of a shutdown.

Influence behaviour · negative incentives · Freedom on the Net, 2020 vs. 2025

Countries where each internet control occurred

What is measured. Each year Freedom House’s country analysts record whether specific “Key Internet Controls” occurred in each assessed country during the coverage period (June to May). The chart counts the countries in which each control was documented at least once. One country can appear in several bars.

The six controls. Social media or messaging platforms blocked; political, social, or religious content blocked; internet or mobile networks deliberately disrupted; pro-government commentators manipulating online discussion; users arrested, imprisoned, or detained for online activity; users physically attacked or killed in connection with online activity.

What the numbers show. The most common control in both years is arresting users: 49 countries in 2020, 57 in 2025. Pro-government commentators rise from 39 to 48, content blocking from 35 to 45, and physical attacks from 32 to 44. Platform blocking rises from 17 to 22. Network disruptions fall from 22 to 17.

Caveats. The 2020 edition assessed 65 countries; 2025 assessed 72, so part of each increase reflects the larger sample. FOTN 2025 dropped three controls tracked in 2020 (new censorship laws, new surveillance laws, technical attacks) for budget reasons. Some 2020 table entries flag events after the coverage period.

Freedom House, Freedom on the Net 2020: The Pandemic’s Digital Shadow, p. 23 (65 countries); Freedom on the Net 2025: An Uncertain Future for the Global Internet, p. 21 (72 countries).

Influence behaviour · negative incentives · CPJ prison census, 2015–2025

Journalists in prison worldwide on December 1

What is measured. The Committee to Protect Journalists counts journalists held in government custody on one day each year, December 1, in connection with their work. It includes reporters, editors, photographers, and people who publish mainly online (bloggers, social media journalists). It excludes those released before December 1 and those held by non-state groups.

What the numbers show. The count rose from 199 in 2015 to 363 in 2022, fell to 320 in 2023, rose to 361 in 2024, and stood at 330 in 2025. In 2025 the leading jailers were China (50, including 7 in Hong Kong), Myanmar (30), Israel (29), Russia (28), Belarus (25), Azerbaijan (24), and Egypt (18).

Caveats. The chart shows each year’s figure as first published. CPJ later revises counts as cases come to light: 2020 to 280, 2021 to 302, 2022 to 370, and 2024 to 384, now the record. A one-day snapshot misses short detentions. The census covers all regime types, not only autocracies.

Committee to Protect Journalists, annual prison censuses, 2015–2025; 2025: “Journalist jailings remain stubbornly high; harsh prison conditions pervasive” (cpj.org).

Influence behaviour · negative incentives · China, 2010–2024

China: public-security vs. national-defense spending, billion yuan

What is measured. Both lines are from China’s official final accounts of general public budget expenditure, as published by the National Bureau of Statistics. “Public security” (公共安全支出) covers domestic order: police, the courts and procuratorates, state security, and (at least until 2018) the People’s Armed Police. “National defense” is the official military budget.

What the numbers show. Public security exceeded defense in every year shown from 2010 to 2020. It rose from 551.8 billion yuan in 2010 to 1,390.2 billion in 2019, then stayed between 1,378 and 1,487 billion (1,479.3 billion in 2024). Defense grew every year, from 533.3 billion to 1,690.9 billion, and has been the larger line since 2021 (in 2021 by only 0.6 billion: 1,378.7 vs. 1,378.1). In Xinjiang, security spending rose 92.8% in a single year, from 30.05 billion yuan in 2016 to 57.95 billion in 2017.

Caveats. In 2018 the People’s Armed Police came under sole military command; whether its budget moved out of the public-security line is not confirmed, so the comparison after 2018 may not be like-for-like. Official defense figures exclude some military-related spending. 2010–2016 values are as compiled by Zenz from NBS data (even years 2010–2014 shown); 2025 final accounts are not yet published.

National Bureau of Statistics, China Statistical Yearbook, table 7-3 (2017–2024). Adrian Zenz, “China’s Domestic Security Spending: An Analysis of Available Data,” China Brief 18(4), Jamestown Foundation, Mar 12, 2018 (2010–2016; Xinjiang).

Influence beliefs · constructive · Oxford Internet Institute, 2020 inventory

Who runs organized social media manipulation: countries, by actor type

What is measured. The Oxford Internet Institute’s inventory reviews news reports, government and think-tank documents, and expert consultations in each country to identify “cyber troops”: government or political-party actors tasked with manipulating public opinion online. In 2020 it found organized manipulation in 81 countries. The chart shows in how many of those countries each type of actor was involved.

What the numbers show. Government agencies (including state-funded media) were involved in 62 countries and political parties in 61. Private firms working for political actors appeared in 48 countries; almost US$60 million was spent hiring them since 2009. Citizens and influencers recruited by campaigns appeared in 51 countries, civil-society groups in 23. Human-run fake accounts were found in 79 countries, automated bots in 57. Disinformation and manipulated media were used in 76 countries, state-sponsored trolling in 59.

Caveats. Categories overlap: one country can have several actor types. The inventory covers democracies as well as autocracies and depends on what has been publicly reported, so countries with less press freedom may be undercounted. 2020 is the most recent edition.

Bradshaw, Bailey & Howard, Industrialized Disinformation: 2020 Global Inventory of Organized Social Media Manipulation, Oxford Internet Institute, Jan 2021: actors pp. 8–9; accounts p. 11; tactics p. 15.

Influence beliefs · constructive · Meta takedowns, 2017–2024

Covert influence networks removed by Meta, by country of origin

What is measured. Meta removes “coordinated inauthentic behavior” (CIB): networks of fake or deceptive accounts that work together to mislead people about who is behind them, on Facebook and Instagram. Each removed network is attributed to a country of origin, which may be a government, a political party, or a private firm working for one.

What the numbers show. Cumulative counts since 2017, as of Meta’s Q3 2024 report: “Russia remains the number one source of global CIB networks we’ve disrupted to date since 2017, with 39 covert influence operations. The next most frequent sources of foreign interference are Iran, with 31 CIB networks, and China, with 11.” In December 2022, Meta reported more than 200 networks from 68 countries since 2017, with Russia at 34 and Iran at 29.

Caveats. Meta publishes only the top sources, not a full table, and has not updated these totals since late 2024. Counts reflect what one company detected and chose to report on its own platforms; one “network” can range from a few accounts to thousands. Networks are counted by origin, not by target.

Meta, Q3 2024 Adversarial Threat Report, Dec 2024 (transparency.meta.com). Meta, “Recapping Our 2022 Coordinated Inauthentic Behavior Enforcements,” Dec 15, 2022.

Influence beliefs · constructive · V-Dem Digital Society Project, 2000–2025

Government use of social media to spread false information at home

What is measured. V-Dem asks: “How often do the government and its agents use social media to disseminate misleading viewpoints or false information to influence its own population?” Answers run from “extremely often” (on all key political issues) to “never, or almost never.” The measurement model turns expert answers into a continuous score; 0 is roughly the global average.

How the chart is built. Sign flipped so that higher means more frequent government disinformation. Lines are unweighted averages for autocracies (closed + electoral) and democracies (electoral + liberal), by year.

What the numbers show. The autocracy average rises from 0.26 in 2000 to 1.13 in 2025; the steepest climb is 2009–2016 (0.50 to 0.99). The democracy average rises from −1.34 to −0.93. 2025 scores (flipped): Russia 2.73, Myanmar 2.72, North Korea 2.62, Iran 2.27, China 2.20, United States 0.66.

Caveats. The question covers a government’s own population only (V-Dem codes foreign-directed disinformation separately). The scale has no natural zero, so differences between years are comparable but ratios are not. Group membership shifts each year; uncertainty bands are not shown.

V-Dem Country-Year Dataset v16 (Coppedge et al. 2026, doi:10.23696/vdemds26), variable v2smgovdom, model point estimates × −1; regime groups from v2x_regime.

Influence beliefs · destructive · V-Dem Digital Society Project, 2000–2025

Government filtering of political information online

What is measured. V-Dem asks: “How frequently does the government censor political information (text, audio, images, or video) on the Internet by filtering (blocking access to certain websites)?” Answers run from “extremely often” to “never, or almost never.” Expert answers are combined into a continuous score.

How the chart is built. Sign flipped so that higher means more filtering. Unweighted averages by regime group and year; 92 autocracies and 87 democracies in 2025.

What the numbers show. Autocracies average 0.28 in 2000 and 0.79 in 2025, with a peak of 0.86 in 2023. Democracies barely move: −1.56 in 2000, −1.44 in 2025. The gap widens from 1.83 to 2.23 points. 2025 scores (flipped): North Korea 3.74, China 2.82, Iran 2.66, Russia 2.00, Myanmar 1.68, United States −1.53.

Caveats. This indicator covers website blocking; deleting individual posts is a separate V-Dem indicator (social media censorship), which follows a similar pattern (autocracies 0.20 → 0.71). Group membership shifts each year; uncertainty bands are not shown.

V-Dem Country-Year Dataset v16 (Coppedge et al. 2026, doi:10.23696/vdemds26), variables v2smgovfilprc and v2smgovsmcenprc, model point estimates × −1; regime groups from v2x_regime.
