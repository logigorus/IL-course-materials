Part I · The ruler · Guriev & Treisman

The key to such regimes, we argue, is the manipulation of information. Rather than terrorizing or indoctrinating the population, rulers survive by leading citizens to believe—rationally but incorrectly—that they are competent and public-spirited. Having won popularity, dictators score points both at home and abroad by mimicking democracy. Guriev & Treisman 2019: 101

Part I · The ruler · Depth · Rozenas & Stukal 2019

Economic news on Russian state television

Bad news is not censored, but it is systematically blamed on external factors, whereas good news is systematically attributed to domestic politicians. Rozenas & Stukal, “How Autocrats Manipulate Economic News,” Journal of Politics 81(3), 2019, abstract
  • Data: daily news reports from Russia’s largest state-owned television network
  • Pattern intensifies around elections and protests, and when leadership approval is high
  • cf. G&T 2019: 117 — the ruler “can also send ‘propaganda’ messages, blaming economic failures on external conditions”

Part III · Across borders · Applebaum · MH17, 2014

After Russian-controlled forces shot down Malaysia Airlines Flight MH17 over Ukraine in 2014, the Russian government reacted not only with a denial, but with multiple stories, plausible and implausible: It blamed the Ukrainian army, and the CIA, and a nefarious plot in which dead people were placed on a plane in order to fake a crash and discredit Russia. This tactic—the so-called fire hose of falsehoods—ultimately produces not outrage but nihilism. Applebaum 2024

“When Soviet leaders lied, they tried to make their falsehoods seem real. They became angry when anyone accused them of lying.”
