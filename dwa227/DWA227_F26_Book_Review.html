The task

Write a review of about 1,000 words of one of the course's three required books:

  • Guriev, Sergei, and Daniel Treisman. Spin Dictators: The Changing Face of Tyranny in the 21st Century. Princeton University Press, 2022.
  • Acemoglu, Daron, and James A. Robinson. The Narrow Corridor: States, Societies, and the Fate of Liberty. Penguin Press, 2019.
  • Applebaum, Anne. Autocracy, Inc.: The Dictators Who Want to Run the World. Doubleday, 2024.

Review the book as a whole rather than only the chapters we have assigned so far. You don't need to read every page with equal care, but your review should show that you know where the argument goes and how the book ends.

A review tells its reader what the book argues and whether the author makes the case, which means that summary is the means and your evaluation is the point. If more than about a third of your review is summary, you have written a book report rather than a review.

Questions to read with

  1. What did the author set out to do: advance an argument, tell a story, overturn conventional wisdom, sound an alarm, or prescribe a policy?
  2. Can you state the main argument in one or two sentences? If you can't, keep reading until you can.
  3. What kind of evidence does the author rely on (cases, data, interviews, history, anecdote), and does it convince you? Where is it strongest, and where is it thinnest?
  4. What does the argument leave out or explain badly, and is there a case you know of that doesn't fit?
  5. Is the book well written, and who is it written for?

A workable structure

You are free to organize the review your own way, though most good reviews move through four parts.

  • Introduction (1 paragraph). The bibliographic information (author, title, publisher, year), a sentence on who the author is, and the question the book takes on, ending with your overall judgment so that the reader knows early where you land.
  • Summary (1–2 paragraphs). The argument and its key moves in your own words, with quotation kept to a minimum and page numbers given for every quotation and for any specific claim you paraphrase.
  • Evaluation (2–3 paragraphs). This is where most of your words belong. Name one strength and one weakness and explain why each matters, asking whether the logic holds and whether the evidence carries the claims the author builds on it, and anchor both judgments in specific passages.
  • Conclusion (1 paragraph). Who should read this book, and what question does it leave open?

What the best reviews do

The strongest reviews I receive put the book in conversation with one other reading from the semester. A few pairings that work well:

  • Spin Dictators against Linz & Stepan's regime typology (Week 3), or against Pei on China (Week 3). Does Xi's China fit the spin model?
  • The Narrow Corridor against Olson's stationary bandit (Week 4), since both try to explain why rulers restrain themselves, or fail to.
  • Autocracy, Inc. against Spin Dictators, which locate the threat in different places, with Guriev and Treisman focused on how individual regimes manage their own publics and Applebaum on the network of regimes that prop each other up. Sharafutdinova & Dawisha (Week 7), on kleptocrats' exit through global finance, make another good partner for Applebaum.

This part is optional, but a single connection developed over a paragraph is the clearest way to show me that you are reading as a student of authoritarianism rather than as a general reader. (If you choose Spin Dictators, keep in mind that Daniel Treisman visits our class on October 29, the day the review is due, so you may get to put your critique to him in person!)

Format

  • Double-spaced, 12-point font, 1-inch margins, numbered pages.
  • Your name, the date, the book under review, and your word count at the top.
  • A title of your own, ideally one that hints at your verdict.
  • Any citation style, as long as you use it consistently. You don't need outside sources, and you are welcome to read published reviews of the book, but cite anything you draw on, because the judgment has to be your own.

AI policy

No AI-generated text, although source-search and brainstorming are fine. Reading the book is the assignment, and this is the same rule that appears in the allowed-use table on the syllabus.

How it's graded (10 points)

Criterion Points What I'm looking for
Understanding of the book 4 You restate the argument accurately and show command of the whole book, not only the opening chapters.
Evaluation 3 You reach a clear judgment and support it with specific evidence from the text, including at least one strength and one weakness rather than praise or complaint alone.
Writing & organization 3 The structure is clear, the verdict appears in the first paragraph, the prose is clean, and quotations and paraphrases carry page numbers.
