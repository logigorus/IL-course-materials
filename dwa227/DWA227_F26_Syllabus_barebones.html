Course Description

Thirty years after Francis Fukuyama proclaimed the “End of History,” political authoritarianism remains viable while democracy is increasingly under threat around the world. In fact, only 7 percent of the global population resides in liberal democracies as of 2025. At the same time, many dictators have embraced hypermobility of capital, free trade, and modern information technology. Deepening globalization has also challenged long-established democratic systems, producing anti-establishment populist politics at the “local” level. How do authoritarian leaders react to the challenges of globalization in the 21st century? Does globalization undermine or enhance their hold on power? Will authoritarianism survive a long-term engagement with global markets? The aim of this course is to explore these and other related questions.

This course fulfills the Global Connections (CPGC) requirement of the college.

Course Objectives

This course covers the political economy of contemporary authoritarianism; how modern autocrats hold power under conditions of global capital mobility, open trade, and networked information; how democracies erode and what global capitalism has to do with it; and what the scholarly literature gets right and wrong about these dynamics.

Student Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish the main analytic categories in the study of non-democratic rule
  2. Summarize and evaluate a scholarly arguments in this literature
  3. Assess measurements on their merits: what democratic backsliding indices claim to measure and where they disagree
  4. Locate, assess, and cite scholarly sources on a research question about authoritarianism, and build an annotated bibliography
  5. Write a 3,000–4,000-word literature review and defend it live under questioning

(CPGC: Global Connections)

Course Structure

Most Tuesday classes begin with an overview of the week’s material; on Thursdays, students work in and out of pre-assigned discussion groups. By Wednesday at 8pm, each student posts 3 questions framed in the context of the readings (about 300–500 words), alternating week to week between two roles. Connector: connect previous readings (and specific passages) to the current week’s readings; discussion questions must include at least three such connections and a question constructed to stimulate discussion. Applier: apply the week’s assigned material to real-world events (contemporary or historical, reported in reputable outlets), producing three such applications and explaining why and how the material applies; applications should stimulate discussion.

Assignments

1. Connector/Applier Posts (10%) Due Wednesday at 8pm each week. Graded complete/incomplete. Two weeks may be skipped, no questions asked. Keep all your posts in a single document.

2. Participation (20%)

  • Fifteen percent: graded on preparation and contribution, not volume. Strong: building on the post rather than reading it aloud, citing a passage, taking up and pushing another student’s point. Adequate: prepared, and speaking on on-deck days. Weak: present but silent, or talking without the text. A mark is recorded for each student at the end of every meeting; cumulative standing is reported once at midterm. Attendance at guest speaker events counts. Students for whom speaking in a group is difficult may tell the instructor in week 2 to arrange other ways to demonstrate preparation.
  • Five percent: attendance at guest-speaker/DWA events. Attend at least two DWA-sponsored events related to global politics or authoritarianism (broadly understood) — including any of the guest speakers listed below — and submit a one-page (single-spaced) response by email within a week of each, naming the talk title and speaker(s), briefly describing the event’s purpose, and connecting it to a theme from the course. Essays are graded complete/not complete; two completed essays earn an A on this part of the participation grade.

Attendance. For absences (illness, family emergencies, official Oxy events, days of faith and conscience), notify the instructor as early as possible to arrange an alternative way to demonstrate the week’s preparation. Students missing more than two weeks’ worth of meetings should meet with the instructor.

3. Two In-Class Quizzes (10%) Short handwritten quizzes: identifications plus one paragraph. - Quiz 1 — Tuesday, September 15 - Quiz 2 — Tuesday, October 27

4. Book Review (10%) About 1000 words, on Spin Dictators, The Narrow Corridor, or Autocracy, Inc. Due: Thursday, October 22.

5. Blue-Book Midterm (15%) In-class, handwritten, Thursday, October 8. The question pool will circulated in advance. One handwritten page of notes allowed.

6. Literature Review with Oral Defense (35%) A 3,000–4,000-word review of the literature on a topic in the academic study of authoritarianism (excluding bibliography) — expanding on a course topic or one of the student’s own choosing, after consulting with the instructor. Three parts: - Annotated bibliography (10%) — due Thursday, November 5 - Panel defense (10%) — November 17, 19, and 24 - Final draft in narrative form (15%) — due Thursday, December 10 (finals period)

Panel defense format. Students are grouped with three or four classmates working on related topics. Each student gives a 4–5 minute statement of their question, their argument, and the weakest point in their own case; the panel then takes 8–10 minutes of open questions. Panelists are expected to have read one another’s bibliographies and to have a question ready for each.

The final draft is graded on a one-page rubric distributed with the assignment, plus a short holistic comment.

Course Policies

Communication

Email replies within 24 hours on weekdays, 48 on weekends. Anonymous written course feedback is collected twice: at the end of week 3 and in the week after the midterm.

Late Assignments

A grace period of 48 hours applies after each deadline, no questions asked. For every 24-hour period after the end of the grace period, one letter grade is deducted. This policy does not apply to the final draft of the literature review, which is due at the deadline.

Laptop & Smartphone Use

Laptops and smartphones may not be used during class (except with accommodations or prior approval); take notes on paper. Bring a laptop for in-class writing and workshop days when asked.

AI Policy

  1. In-class work (discussion, quizzes, the blue-book midterm, presentations and defenses): closed-book except where stated, and no AI.
  2. Take-home writing: written without generative AI. AI may be used to search for sources, verify facts, or brainstorm — testing an idea, working through an argument, sketching an outline — but no AI-generated text may be submitted as your own writing; you are responsible for whatever appears on the page with your name on it.
  3. No AI detectors are used. If work raises authorship concerns, the student will be asked to walk through their argument in conversation.
  4. When in doubt, ask first.

Statement of the Shared Academic Integrity Commitment

Academic Integrity is a shared community value. It is built around trust and respect between members of the Occidental Community, and embodies a commitment to honesty and integrity in every aspect of one’s academic life.

All members of the Occidental community are committed to uphold the highest degree of academic integrity. Unless stipulated otherwise, the academic work done for all assignments is expected to be the student’s own; students are expected to give proper credit to the ideas and work of others.

Signing the Academic Integrity Commitment at the beginning of every semester represents a student’s affirmation to uphold the shared values of honesty and integrity. When signing the Integrity Commitment associated with work in a course, students are affirming that they have not cheated, plagiarized, fabricated, or falsified information; nor assisted others in these actions.

Refer to Student Handbook for the framing of the commitment, definitions of Academic Ethics, and Process for alleged violations of the commitment.
