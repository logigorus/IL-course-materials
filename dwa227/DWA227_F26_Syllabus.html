The assignments

1. Connector/Applier Posts (10%)
Your weekly discussion questions, due Wednesday at 8pm, graded complete/incomplete as the semester goes. You may skip two weeks, no questions asked. I review them Thursday morning and bring what I find into the room — I don't write comments back, because the discussion is the feedback. Posts are your ticket to discussion; you'll be asked to build on them live.

2. Participation (15%)
I grade preparation and contribution, not volume. Strong: you build on your post rather than read it aloud, you cite a passage, you take up someone else's point and push it. Adequate: prepared, and speaking on your on-deck days. Weak: present but silent, or talking without the text. I record a mark for each of you at the end of every meeting, in the room — I don't reconstruct participation in December. You'll get your cumulative standing once at midterm, in time to change it. Speaker event attendance counts. If speaking in a group is hard for you, tell me in week 2 — there are other ways to be visibly prepared and I'll use them.

Attendance. Come to class; this course is a seminar and the work we do in the room can't be made up from notes. Life happens — illness, family emergencies, official Oxy events, days of faith and conscience. Tell me as early as you can and we'll find another way for you to demonstrate the week's preparation. If you're missing more than two weeks' worth of meetings, come talk to me about whether this is the right semester for the course.

3. Two In-Class Quizzes (10%)
Short handwritten quizzes: identifications plus one paragraph. Ideas count, penmanship doesn't. A makeup slot is available for documented conflicts. One weak quiz should not decide your semester: I count the higher of your two scores double and the lower half. - Quiz 1 — Tuesday, September 15 (moved off Thursday to clear the Glebov visit) - Quiz 2 — Tuesday, October 27 (fixed: the Thu Oct 29 guest visit is confirmed, so the quiz stays on Tuesday.)

4. Book Review (10%)
In about 1000 words, review Spin Dictators, The Narrow Corridor, or Autocracy, Inc. Due: Thursday, October 22.

5. Blue-Book Midterm (15%)
In-class, handwritten, Thursday, October 8. The question pool is circulated two weeks in advance (Sep 24); exam questions are drawn from the pool, and the exam draws heavily on the three required books. You may bring one handwritten page of notes. We will hold an ungraded practice session early in the term (Thu Sep 3).

6. Literature Review with Oral Defense (40%)
The main writing assignment is a 3,000–4,000-word review of the literature on a topic in the academic study of authoritarianism (excluding bibliography) — expanding on a course topic or one of your own choosing, after consulting with me. Three parts: - Annotated bibliography (10%) — due Thursday, November 5 - Panel defense (10%) — November 17, 19, and 24 - Final draft in narrative form (20%) — due Thursday, December 10 (finals period)

How the panel defense works. You'll be grouped with three or four classmates working on related topics. Each of you gives a 4–5 minute statement of your question, your argument, and the weakest point in your own case. Then the panel takes 8–10 minutes of open questions — from me and from the room, and from each other. You are expected to have read your panelists' bibliographies and to have a question ready for each of them. I grade live, on a one-page rubric, during the session.

This is harder than reading a paper aloud and it's the point of the course: defending a claim you built, in real time, to people who know the material. It is also the one assessment that cannot be outsourced to anyone or anything.

A word on feedback. You get detailed comments three times, where you can still act on them: on the annotated bibliography, in the Week 12 workshop, and in the panel questions. The final draft is graded on a one-page rubric, distributed with the assignment, plus a short holistic comment. Comments written after the course ends are comments nobody can use. If you want them anyway, ask me in January and you'll have them.

Recovery. The midterm question pool goes out two weeks early because I want you preparing against known questions, not guessing. After the midterm I'll send each of you a short read on where you stand and what to do about it, early enough to act on.

Course Structure

I will begin most Tuesday classes with an overview of the material for the week. On Thursdays, students will work in and out of pre-assigned discussion groups. By Wednesday at 8pm, each student will need to post 3 questions framed in the context of the readings (about 300–500 words). From week to week, you will alternate between two roles:

Post your questions to the shared Connector/Applier doc under that week's heading — see the doc for exact formatting instructions.

Connector — Your job is to ensure a continuous semester-long conversation rather than a series of reviews of individual readings. Connect previous readings (and specific passages) to the current week's readings. Your discussion questions should include at least three such connections. Construct a question that will stimulate discussion.

Applier — Apply the material assigned for the given week to real-world events (contemporary or historical, reported in reputable outlets). For each week, produce three such applications. Explain why and how the material applies. Your applications should stimulate discussion and even disagreement among your group members.

About the course

Course Description

Thirty years after Francis Fukuyama proclaimed the "End of History," political authoritarianism remains viable while democracy is increasingly under threat around the world. In fact, only 7 percent of the global population resides in liberal democracies as of 2025. At the same time, many dictators have embraced hypermobility of capital, free trade, and modern information technology. Deepening globalization has also challenged long-established democratic systems, producing anti-establishment populist politics at the "local" level.

How do authoritarian leaders react to the challenges of globalization in the 21st century? Does globalization undermine or enhance their hold on power? Will authoritarianism survive a long-term engagement with global markets? The aim of this course is to explore these and other related questions.

This course fulfills the Global Connections (CPGC) requirement of the college.

Course Objectives

This course covers the political economy of contemporary authoritarianism; how modern autocrats hold power under conditions of global capital mobility, open trade, and networked information; how democracies erode; and what the scholarly literature can and cannot yet establish about either.

Student Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you will be able to:

  1. Distinguish the main analytic categories in the study of non-democratic rule — totalitarian, authoritarian, personalist, "spin" — and say what evidence would place a given regime in one rather than another
  2. Summarize and evaluate a scholarly argument in this literature — its question, claim, evidence, and weakest point — in writing and out loud, without notes
  3. Assess a measurement dispute on its merits: what democratic backsliding indices claim to measure, where they disagree, and why it matters
  4. Locate, assess, and cite scholarly sources on a research question about authoritarianism, and build an annotated bibliography that separates strong evidence from weak
  5. Write a 3,000–4,000-word literature review and defend it live under questioning (CPGC: Global Connections)

Course Workload

DWA 227 is a 4-unit course. On average, you should expect to spend at least twelve (12) hours a week (including in-class time) on this course.

Use of AI tools

Why this course is (mostly) an AI-free zone

This course teaches you to do something AI cannot do for you: build and defend your own causal arguments about politics and the world economy. That skill — reading closely, weighing evidence, constructing an argument, and standing behind it in real time — develops only through practice, and the practice only works if the thinking is yours. The friction is the point.

I know AI tools are everywhere, and you will use them elsewhere — in other courses, in jobs, in life. I use them in my own research and administrative work, as an expert checking a tool's output against knowledge I already have. You are here to build that knowledge first. That's not hypocrisy; that's sequencing.

Some of you took DWA 103 with me last spring, when this course ran the other experiment: AI as a collaborative partner, Gemini workshops and all, as part of the college's AI Institute pilot. This isn't a contradiction — it's the same experiment, continued. That semester taught me (and, judging by your feedback, many of you) that AI is genuinely useful for simulating expert workflows, and that it quietly eats the practice reps that build expertise in the first place. So this year the design changes: the tools go away where the learning happens, and the grade weight moves into the room. I'll be just as interested in your feedback on this version.

So this course puts its grade weight where your unaided thinking is visible: in-class discussion, handwritten quizzes and exams, and oral presentation. Take-home writing is deliberately lower-stakes and structured in stages, so the work — and the learning — happens in a process I can see.

The ground rules

  1. In-class work (discussion, quizzes, the blue-book midterm, presentations and defenses): closed-book except where stated, and no AI — it's you, paper, and the room.
  2. Take-home writing: I expect it to be written without generative AI — no drafting, rewriting, summarizing of readings, or outline generation by AI. You can use AI to search for sources, verify facts, or brainstorm — testing an idea, working through an argument, sketching an outline — but the writing on the page has to be yours. I am direct about the fact that I cannot verify this, which is exactly why these assignments carry modest weight and are built in stages. Doing them yourself is what prepares you for the parts of the course that carry the grade.
  3. No detectors, ever. I will never run your work through an AI detector — they are unreliable and biased against non-native speakers and neurodivergent writers. If something in your work doesn't sound like you, my response is a conversation: I'll ask you to walk me through your argument. Being able to explain your own work is the standard — and it's one you should welcome.
  4. When in doubt, ask first. A two-minute question before the fact is never an integrity problem. We'll discuss this policy together in week 1 — I want your honest read on it.

Academic Integrity

Statement of the Shared Academic Integrity Commitment

Academic Integrity is a shared community value. It is built around trust and respect between members of the Occidental Community, and embodies a commitment to honesty and integrity in every aspect of one's academic life.

All members of the Occidental community are committed to uphold the highest degree of academic integrity. Unless stipulated otherwise, the academic work done for all assignments is expected to be the student's own; students are expected to give proper credit to the ideas and work of others.

Signing the Academic Integrity Commitment at the beginning of every semester represents a student's affirmation to uphold the shared values of honesty and integrity. When signing the Integrity Commitment associated with work in a course, students are affirming that they have not cheated, plagiarized, fabricated, or falsified information; nor assisted others in these actions.

Refer to Student Handbook for the framing of the commitment, definitions of Academic Ethics, and Process for alleged violations of the commitment.

And a word from me

That's the college's language, and it governs. Here's the part I care about more: people cut corners when they feel underprepared or out of time, not because they woke up wanting to be dishonest. I can help with both if you come early — I would much rather spend that hour with you than the one after a misconduct report. Unsure whether something counts as a source, or whether a form of help is allowed? Ask. Asking first is never the problem.

Mid-semester reports and incompletes

Mid-semester progress reports. Mid-semester progress reports are not intended to be used as determinative or predictive of the final grade in a course, but to provide an opportunity for the faculty to communicate an approximate estimation of progress by the students in the course by the midpoint of the current semester. I will submit yours during the Fall 2026 window (October 5–18) — after the midterm, so it reflects real information. Please look at it before you meet with your academic advisor.

Incompletes. You are eligible for an incomplete if you have completed at least one-third of the graded work, that work is of passing quality, and the Dean of Students Office has verified a personal circumstance that prevented you from finishing. Even then, the decision is mine. In this course an incomplete makes sense only when the outstanding work is the final paper — discussion, posts, quizzes, the midterm, and the panel defense can't be reconstructed after the semester ends. If you're falling behind before the withdrawal deadline, come talk to me; withdrawing is often the better outcome.

Civil Rights & Title IX

In the event that you write or speak about having experienced discrimination or harassment on the basis of a protected characteristic or sexual misconduct (including sexual assault, dating/domestic violence, stalking, sexual exploitation or any other form of sexual and/or gender-based harassment), as a designated Responsible Employee, I must inform the Civil Rights & Title IX Office. They will contact you to let you know about resources and support services at Oxy, as well as reporting options both on and off-campus. You have no obligation to respond to the Civil Rights & Title IX Office or to meet with them to discuss support services and reporting options.

If you do not want the Civil Rights & Title IX Office contacted, instead of disclosing this information to your instructor, either through conversation or a class assignment, you can speak confidentially with:

  • Oxy's Survivor Advocate, Project SAFE (survivoradvocate@oxy.edu)
  • Emmons Counseling (For appointments, call: 323-259-2657)
  • Rev. Dr. Susan Young, Office of Religious and Spiritual Life (young@oxy.edu)
  • Oxy 24/7 Confidential Hotline (323-341-4141)

The College's civil rights policies, along with additional resources, can be found at: https://www.oxy.edu/civil-rights-title-ix. If you would like to contact the Civil Rights & Title IX Office directly, you may email Civil Rights & Title IX Coordinator Alexandra Fulcher at afulcher@oxy.edu or call 323-259-1338.

Special Accommodations/Learning Differences

Occidental College (Oxy) complies with the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 as amended, Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 as amended, and other applicable state and federal law prohibiting discrimination against individuals with disabilities.

  • All accommodation requests including academic, housing (ESA & Services Animal) and temporary accommodations are managed by the Disability Services Office. It is a student's responsibility to request accommodations via the Disability Services website. For information about additional accommodations and support services, students can email accessibility@oxy.edu.
  • Oxy's Psychological Testing Program: We offer low cost psychological testing for students who qualify. Any student who thinks they may have a learning, or psychological disability may complete Oxy's Psych Testing Application to be added to the waitlist.
  • Academic Success Coaching: Our Academic Success Coach assists students in developing skills and strategies to enhance their academic performance. They provide personalized guidance on time management, organization, study techniques, goal setting, and self-regulation. By collaborating with students, our coach helps identify and overcome obstacles to academic success, fostering a supportive environment that promotes personal and educational growth. Contact: academiccoaching@oxy.edu

If something about how this course is designed or taught is getting in the way of your doing your best work, tell me — with or without a formal accommodation. I can often fix it. This applies to the panel defense in particular: if speaking under questioning is a documented barrier for you, come see me in the first two weeks and we'll design an equivalent.

Accommodations for Reasons of Faith and Conscience

Consistent with Occidental College's commitment to creating an academic community that is respectful of and welcoming to persons of differing backgrounds, we believe that students should be excused from class for reasons of faith and conscience without academic consequence. While it is not feasible to schedule coursework around all days of conviction for a class as a whole, faculty will honor requests from individual students to reschedule coursework, to be absent from classes that conflict with the identified days. Information about this policy and a religious and spiritual holiday calendar are available on the ORSL website.
