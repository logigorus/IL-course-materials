Introduction

In 2004, at the peak of the global AIDS epidemic, 2.1 million people died from HIV-related illnesses, the vast majority in developing countries where patented antiretroviral drugs cost more than most governments spent on healthcare per capita (UNAIDS, 2023). The medications existed to save these lives. The barrier was price: pharmaceutical companies charged $10,000–15,000 per patient annually for treatments that cost under $200 to produce generically (Médecins Sans Frontières, 2001). Yet by 2010, deaths had fallen to 1.4 million, and today over 31 million people access antiretroviral therapy worldwide (UNAIDS, 2023). What changed was not the science but the politics: a handful of developing countries found ways to break pharmaceutical monopolies and secure affordable medicines.

Research Question: What conditions enabled successful compulsory licensing of antiretroviral drugs in some countries but not others, and what do these conditions reveal about the governance frameworks needed for equitable access to Lenacapavir?

Framework & Methodology — INUS Conditions and MSSD

The framework identifies four conditions as individually necessary and jointly sufficient (INUS conditions, following Mackie 1965): (1) International Legal Legitimacy, (2a) Organized Civil Society Advocacy, (2b) Government Receptivity to Evidence-Based Policy, and (3) Domestic Technical Capacity for Generic Production.

The Most Similar Systems Design controls for WTO membership, TRIPS obligations, Doha access, epidemic severity, and civil society presence, allowing the variation in specific enabling conditions to explain outcome variation.

[Framework section continues with the logic of disaggregating condition 2 into 2a and 2b, and the justification for each condition drawn from the literature...]

★ Strength — INUS Framework Is Well-Chosen and Well-Applied
Using Mackie's INUS logic to structure your conditions is sophisticated and analytically productive. The disaggregation of "civil society" into organized advocacy (2a) and government receptivity (2b) is the paper's most original methodological contribution — it resolves what would otherwise be an anomaly in the South Africa case. This is exactly the kind of move that demonstrates you're not just applying a method but thinking with it.
✎ Revision — Make the MSSD Limitations Explicit Earlier

✎ Revision — Define "Success" and "Failure" as Outcomes
What to do: Your dependent variable is the outcome of compulsory licensing efforts, but you should define explicitly what counts as "success" and "failure." Brazil saved $1.2 billion through a threat strategy and didn't issue a CL for six years — is that success? Thailand issued six CLs but absorbed significant retaliation. Gilead Sciences charges over $40,000 per patient-year in the United States; Hill and colleagues document that generic production at scale could be achieved for under $100 per year, a 400-fold markup (Hill et al., 2025, p. 548).

The License: On October 2, 2024, Gilead announced a voluntary license for lenacapavir covering six generic manufacturers and 120 countries. Brazil, Argentina, Colombia, Mexico, Peru, Russia, China, Thailand, and most of the Middle East and North Africa were excluded...

The Diversion Clause: ...the six licensed manufacturers are contractually prohibited from supplying lenacapavir to countries that have issued compulsory licenses if those countries are not in the licensed territory. An excluded country that issues a CL cannot legally obtain supply from any of the six manufacturers most technically positioned to provide it.
