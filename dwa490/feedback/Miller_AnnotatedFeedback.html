Introduction

"I will wear costumes, after this war will finish" — Vlodymyr Zelenskyy

We have a shallow understanding that clothing has functioned as an avenue for personal expression in society. Hosting a fashion week or exporting haute couture may seem trivial conversations to "real diplomats", who engage in "tangible" negotiations that actually make a difference — advance national interest or avoiding war. Undeniable still is fashion's interaction with many industries, including diplomacy. Regardless of the contexts it always operates as an indicator for identity, authority, and values.

Fashion however evolves from pure symbolism or signaling when contextualized in international relations. It becomes a tool. Fashion can influence perception, legitimacy and international reputation. Yes it can happen on an individual, state leader scale, like when Zeleinsky's track suit was interpreted as disrespect towards the Trump administration. But its reputation can economically advantage a nation, like when Japan's "Cool Japan" government initiative to revitalize the economy through exports of cosmopolitan ideas of Japanese fashion.

In an era where global competition increasingly depends on cultural attraction and reputational capital rather than military might, fashion emerges as a potentially significant instrument within the broader framework of soft power.

Soft power may be first coined by Joseph S Nye as the ability of states to shape the preferences of others through attraction rather than coercion. Unlike hard power, which relies on military force or economic pressure, soft power operates through culture, values, and legitimacy. Soft power situates cultural industries in high importance within contemporary international relations, as they can generate influence by shaping how nations are perceived abroad.

Fashion is therefore simultaneously symbolic and economic: a cultural artifact capable of communicating identity while also operating within global markets of production, consumption, and exchange. Fashion is Diplomacy.

Despite fashion's cultural visibility, it has only recently begun to receive attention within scholarship on diplomacy and international relations. Research on fashion diplomacy often centers on historically dominant fashion capitals, particularly France, where state support for haute couture helped establish Paris as an economic force, a symbol of style and prestige. However, limiting the discussion to established Western fashion systems obscures the ways in which emerging fashion industries may also contribute to national reputation and economic diplomacy.

This paper contends that many nations, particularly of the global south, has the capacity to use fashion diplomacy to advance their interests, economic or not, and will use South Africa as prime case study.

Literature Review — Nation Branding & Competitive Identity

Soft power would remain an abstract concept of Public Diplomacy without strategy. The literature turns towards nation branding. It should be noted that the contemporary understanding of "brand" is problematic to apply to international relations theory. This implies that nations operate like a company selling a product. But they don't, countries only seek to protect and advance themselves...

As Simon Anholt argues, countries are not consumer goods: they are not for sale, not easily mistaken for one another, and do not "come in wrappers." The principles of commercial branding do not simply transfer. Anholt further distinguishes the three meanings of "brand." 1) Designed identity (logos, communications), 2) Organizational culture 3) Reputation in the minds of target audiences.

It is his third definition that matters most in International Relations. Countries are judged by what they do, not what they say...

This forms the basis of Anholt's theory of Competitive Identity, which mimics a fusion of realism's natural competitive nature in an anarchic world order, and liberalism where cooperation, shared norms, and interdependence allow states to enhance their image and influence through non-coercive means.

Florian Kaefer, in Country Branding: A Practitioner Perspective, reinforces this constructivist lens. She argues that country brands are values based platforms that represent identity and intentions.

Both Anholt and Kaefer keep stressing the importance of not thinking of this in terms of traditional branding and symbolic actions used in business. "Logos or slogans are not brands. They are communication tools." Essentially, no glossy campaign will earn respect. This is a far cry from professor Jay Wang's understanding of Nation Branding. In Shaping China's Global Imagination, Jian Wang argues that mega-events like World Expos function as strategic platforms of public diplomacy... Anholt would criticize Jay Wang for writing a book about how world expo presentations contribute to a nation's public image... He would say that there are no real world outcomes for the presenting nation, as this is more like an empty marketing campaign.

This is the framework for the central question of this paper.

If fashion is a major cultural product, can it function as a substantive mechanism of competitive identity rather than superficial branding?
