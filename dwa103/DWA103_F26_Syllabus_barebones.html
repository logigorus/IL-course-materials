Course Description

An undergraduate survey of the field of Global Political Economy (GPE), required for Diplomacy and World Affairs majors and intended for students with some background in International Relations and Economics. The first part of the course covers the foundational theories of global political economy; the second explores globalization with a focus on trade, finance, energy, and economic development. During the second half of the semester, students write a literature review project on a topic of their choice. This course fulfills the Global Connections (CPGC) requirement of the college.

Course Objectives

This course covers the major theoretical traditions in global political economy (liberalism, realism/mercantilism, constructivism, and Marxism) and then applies them to trade, finance, energy, and development. Students acquire research and writing skills by preparing a literature review assignment on a topic of their choice.

Student Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you will be able to:

  1. Identify and contrast the core claims of the four main GPE traditions, and say what evidence would count for or against each
  2. Summarize and evaluate a scholarly argument in the field (claims, evidence, and assumptions) in writing and out loud
  3. Locate, assess, and cite scholarly sources on a GPE topic
  4. Write a 2,500–3,000-word literature review that organizes a body of scholarship around a research puzzle in the conventions of the social sciences
  5. Apply a GPE framework to current or past events and defend the application

Course Structure

Tuesday classes generally begin with an overview of the week’s material. Thursdays are devoted to discussion in groups. Students come prepared with discussion questions framed around the readings, alternating between two roles from week to week. Connector — connects previous readings (and specific passages) to the current week’s material and constructs a question to stimulate discussion. Applier — applies the week’s material to real-world events (contemporary or historical, reported in reputable outlets), explaining why and how the framework applies. Keep all discussion questions in one document (e.g., a Google Doc); they may be reviewed during the semester.

Assignments

1. Participation (25%)

  • About 20 percent: active and productive participation in class discussion. Strong participation builds on prepared notes rather than reading them aloud, cites passages, and takes up others’ points; adequate participation is prepared and speaking but stays on the talking points; weak participation is silent presence or talking without prepared questions. Attendance at relevant campus speaker events counts toward participation.
  • Five percent: attendance at DWA events. Attend at least two DWA-sponsored events related to global politics (broadly understood) and submit a one-page (single-spaced) response by email within a week of each — naming the talk title and speaker(s), briefly describing the event’s purpose, and connecting it to a theme from the course. Essays are graded complete/not complete; two completed essays earn an A on this part of the participation grade.

Attendance. Attendance at class meetings is required. Absences for illness, family emergencies, or days of faith and conscience may be excused: notify the professor as early as possible to arrange another way to demonstrate the week’s preparation. Missing more than two weeks’ worth of meetings will affect your grade.

2. Two In-Class Quizzes (10%)

Short handwritten quizzes: a few identifications plus one paragraph, in the first 10–15 minutes of class. The higher of the two scores counts double; the lower counts half. Quiz 1: Thursday, September 17. Quiz 2: Thursday, October 29.

3. Blue-Book Midterm (25%)

In-class, handwritten, Thursday, October 8 (before Fall Break). The question pool will be circulated in advance (Thursday, September 24).

4. Literature Review (40%)

A 2500-3000-word review of the literature on a GPE topic (excluding bibliography), expanding on a course topic or one of your own choosing, after consulting with the professor. Example topics: China–US rivalry; global kleptocracy; the green/circular economy; gender and the global economy; corporate citizenship and responsibility; the rise of populism/authoritarianism; the sustainable development goals (SDGs); social and economic rights. Three parts:

  • Description & annotated bibliography (10%) — due Thursday, November 5
  • Presentation (10%) — week of November 17 (5 minutes each, hard stop, plus questions)
  • Final draft in narrative form (20%) — due Thursday, December 10 (finals period); no 48-hour extension; graded on the rubric distributed with the assignment

Course Policies

Communication

Email replies within 24 hours on weekdays, 48 on weekends. If you have not heard back after that, follow up.

Late Assignments

48-hour grace period after each deadline, no questions asked. For every 24-hour period after the end of the grace period, a letter grade is deducted. This policy does not apply to the final draft of the literature review.

Laptop & Smartphone Use

Laptops and smartphones may not be used during lectures (except with accommodations or prior approval); take notes on paper. Bring your laptop for in-class writing and discussion work; you will be told when to use it.

AI Policy

  1. In-class work (discussion, quizzes, the blue-book midterm, presentations): closed-book except where stated, and no AI.
  2. Take-home writing: written without generative AI. AI may be used to learn, look for sources, verify data, or generate illustrations, but you are responsible for whatever appears on the page with your name on it.
  3. No detectors. Work will never be run through an AI detector. If something in your work doesn’t sound like you, you will be asked to walk through your argument in conversation.
  4. When in doubt, ask first.

Statement of the Shared Academic Integrity Commitment

Academic Integrity is a shared community value. It is built around trust and respect between members of the Occidental Community, and embodies a commitment to honesty and integrity in every aspect of one’s academic life.

All members of the Occidental community are committed to uphold the highest degree of academic integrity. Unless stipulated otherwise, the academic work done for all assignments is expected to be the student’s own; students are expected to give proper credit to the ideas and work of others.

Signing the Academic Integrity Commitment at the beginning of every semester represents a student’s affirmation to uphold the shared values of honesty and integrity. When signing the Integrity Commitment associated with work in a course, students are affirming that they have not cheated, plagiarized, fabricated, or falsified information; nor assisted others in these actions.

Refer to Student Handbook for the framing of the commitment, definitions of Academic Ethics, and Process for alleged violations of the commitment.
