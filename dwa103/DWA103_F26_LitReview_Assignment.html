Overview

The main writing assignment in this course is a 2,500–3,000-word review of the scholarly literature on a topic in global political economy (the bibliography does not count toward the word limit). You can expand on a topic from the course or choose one of your own, after consulting with me. You will find the scholarly conversation on that topic, organize it, and explain what scholars have learned, where they disagree, and what remains unresolved.

The project is built in stages on purpose. Each stage gets feedback before the next one is due, and the presentation lets you show in person that you know your literature.

What a literature review is — and is not

A literature review surveys what scholars have found on a question: where they agree, where they disagree, and why. It then evaluates the state of the field: what is well established, what is contested, what is missing, and what kind of evidence would settle it.

It is not a book report (a summary of source 1, then source 2, then source 3). It is not an op-ed arguing for a policy. And it is not a research paper testing your own hypothesis. Your own voice comes through in how you organize the field and in how you judge it.

Choosing a topic

Pick one of the ten topics below, or propose your own. A topic of your own is welcome, especially if it lets you go deeper into something we only touched on in class or covers something the course leaves out (for example, gender and the global economy, corporate responsibility, the SDGs, global kleptocracy, or social and economic rights). Either way, talk it through with me before you submit Part 1. Come to office hours or send me a short email with the topic and one or two questions you want to understand. Doing this by Thursday, October 29 leaves you a full week to build the bibliography.

A good topic is narrow enough to read seriously in a few weeks, and broad enough that scholars have actually argued about it. “China” is too broad. “Did China’s WTO accession cost US manufacturing jobs, and who lost?” is a literature.

⚠️ Starter readings, not required readings.

You do not need to use all 10 readings listed under your topic. Your final bibliography should contain 8–10 sources that you have identified as most relevant to your specific topic. Some may come from this list; others you will find through your own Google Scholar research. The search process is part of the assignment — follow citation trails, and check recent Annual Reviews for orientation.

Part 1 — Description & Annotated Bibliography

Due Thursday, November 5, 11:59 pm · 10% of the final grade · one PDF or Word file by email

Part 1 stakes out your topic, surveys the first 5–7 key sources, and starts you thinking critically about the field. It has three components, in this order, in one document.

What counts as a scholarly source?

Prioritize peer-reviewed journal articles in political science, economics, sociology and related fields, and books or chapters from university presses. Working papers from recognized research institutions (NBER, the World Bank, the IMF, the Peterson Institute) are acceptable in moderation. News articles, opinion pieces, think-tank blog posts, Wikipedia and textbooks are not scholarly sources. You may use them for a fact or a statistic in your description, but they do not count toward your 5–7 sources.

Step 1 — Topic description (~500 words)

  1. Define the issue. What research problem are you surveying? Frame it as an area of scholarly debate, not a hypothesis you plan to test.
  2. Establish the context. What are the broad trends? Give the reader a few facts, figures or indicators that show the scope and scale of the issue (trade volumes, growth rates, inequality measures, capital flows), with sources.
  3. Identify the actors. Who are the key players — states, international organizations, firms, social movements — and why do their choices matter?
  4. Motivate the topic. Why should anyone care? Connect the issue to a policy debate, a current event, or an unresolved scholarly disagreement. Answer the “so what?” question.

Step 2 — Annotated bibliography (5–7 scholarly sources)

For each source:

  1. Full citation in Chicago author-date style, used consistently.
  2. Summary (2–3 sentences). What is the author’s main argument or finding? Be specific: name the thesis, the evidence or method, and the key result. “This article is about trade” is not a summary.
  3. Critical engagement (2–3 sentences). How does this source bear on your topic? What is most useful about it, and where are its limits? Note specific pages, figures or quotations you expect to come back to.
One annotated entry, as an example

Korzeniewicz, Miguel. 2015. “Commodity Chains and Marketing Strategies: Nike and the Global Athletic Footwear Industry.” In The Globalization Reader, edited by Frank J. Lechner and John Boli, 175–85. Chichester: Wiley.

Summary: Korzeniewicz analyzes the design, production, distribution and marketing of athletic footwear, using Nike as a case study. The article maps Nike’s commodity chain across “core” and “noncore” societies, showing how value is captured at the design and branding stages rather than in manufacturing.

Critical engagement: Linking production and consumption within a single commodity chain is a powerful analytical move, but the article is dated. Rising consumer demand in emerging markets, and authoritarian states courting global brands, complicate the framework. See especially p. 178 for evidence relevant to labor-rights debates.

Step 3 — Reflection (~500 words)

Drawing on the sources you annotated, write a short reflective essay that addresses:

  • Themes. What patterns, points of agreement, or recurring findings appear across your sources? Are there identifiable camps or schools of thought?
  • Gaps and limitations. Where is the research thin, outdated, or narrow (one region, one period, one kind of data)? What remains unanswered?
  • Research questions. Based on those gaps and tensions, pose 2–3 research questions a future project could pursue. You are not committing to answer them. The point is to show that reading the literature turned up real, answerable questions.
Finding sources efficiently

Start from the starter readings or a recent review article (the Annual Review of Political Science and the Journal of Economic Literature are good places to look), then follow citation trails in both directions: what a good article cites, and what cites it. In Google Scholar, “Cited by” does the second.

Weigh recency and influence. A field’s seminal works are the ones everyone cites; its frontier is in the last five years. You need both.

Use a reference manager. Zotero is free and will format your Chicago citations for you.

What you get back: written comments on your topic, your sources and your research questions, as soon as I can, ideally before the workshops. This is the stage where a topic that is too broad, too narrow, or short on scholarship gets fixed.

Part 2 — Presentation

Tuesday, November 17; Thursday, November 19; Tuesday, November 24 · 10% of the final grade · in class · running order in the shared doc

You will present your literature review to the class in five minutes, with a hard stop, followed by questions from your classmates and me. This is a draft-stage presentation: you are not expected to have a finished paper. You are expected to know your literature, and to be able to answer questions about it without reading from your notes.

What to cover in five minutes

  1. The question and why it matters (one minute, with one fact or figure).
  2. Two or three themes or camps in the literature, naming the key authors on each side and what they found.
  3. One live disagreement: where the scholarship conflicts, and why (different data, different cases, different assumptions).
  4. One gap or open question, and where your final draft is going.
Format

Up to five slides (optional). Use them for a figure, a table, or a map of the camps in your literature, not for text you read aloud. Time yourself beforehand: I will stop you at five minutes, wherever you are. Questions follow, about three minutes per presenter. The presentation is live and in person: no AI.

Part 3 — Final Literature Review

Due Thursday, December 10, 11:59 pm (finals period) · 20% of the final grade · no grace period · one PDF or Word file by email

The final paper is a 2,500–3,000-word essay in narrative form, not counting the bibliography. It should read as a coherent account of a scholarly conversation: who said what, where they agree, where they disagree, and what remains unresolved. It is not a list of article summaries. The bibliography should contain 8–10 scholarly sources that you have judged most relevant to your topic. Some will come from Part 1; others you will find after the feedback and workshops.

Suggested structure

  1. Introduction (~500 words). Define the issue, give the reader a reason to care (with data or indicators), state the question that organizes the field, and preview the structure of the paper.
  2. The scholarly conversation (~1,500–1,700 words). The heart of the paper. Organize the literature thematically, around 2–4 themes, debates or findings, and show how different scholars contribute to each. Use specific evidence (findings, data, page numbers). Show that you can tell sources apart: not every study is equally rigorous or equally relevant.
  3. Conclusion (~500–800 words). Your own voice is strongest here. What is established? What is still puzzling or missing? How should the field refocus, and what would advance it — new data, new methods, new cases? One productive option: bring one of the course’s traditions (liberalism, realism/mercantilism, constructivism, Marxism) into the discussion as a lens. What does it reveal about the assumptions or blind spots of the literature you surveyed? This is offered as a frame, not a requirement.

Common pitfalls

The book-report structure. Article 1, then Article 2, then Article 3. Organize by theme, not by source.

Weak sources. Textbooks, news articles and non-peer-reviewed material (working papers from the institutions listed above excepted) signal a shallow engagement with the field.

Treating every study as equal. A large cross-national study and a single-case opinion piece are not the same kind of evidence. Say which is which.

A conclusion that only summarizes. The conclusion should judge the field, not repeat the paper.

AI policy for this assignment

Annotated bibliography and final draft: take-home, no use of AI to generate text. You are welcome to use AI to learn, look for sources and verify data, but you are always responsible for whatever winds up on the page with your name on it.

Presentation: in class, oral, no AI.

I will never run your work through an AI detector. If something in your work doesn’t sound like you, we will have a conversation, and I will ask you to walk me through your argument. That is one reason the project includes a presentation.
