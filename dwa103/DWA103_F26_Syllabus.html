The assignments

1. Participation (25%)

About 20 percent will come from active and productive participation in class. Strong: you build on your notes rather than read them aloud, you cite a passage, you take up someone else's point and push it. Adequate: prepared, and speaking, but sticking to the talking points only. Weak: present but silent, or talking without prepared questions. Attendance at relevant campus speaker events counts a great deal - we have lots of fascinating events this semester. You are the intended audience for these speakers.

Five percent will come from attendance at DWA events. Over the semester, attend at least two DWA-sponsored events related to global politics (broadly understood) and submit a one-page (single-spaced) response by email within a week of each — naming the talk title and speaker(s), briefly describing the event's purpose, and connecting it to a theme from the course. Essays are graded complete/not complete; two completed essays earn an A on this part of the participation grade.

Attendance. You must attend class meetings. Life happens, such as illness, family emergencies, days of faith and conscience. Tell me as early as you can and we'll find another way for you to demonstrate the week's preparation. What I can't do is grade a semester of absences generously at the end, so if you're missing more than two weeks' worth of meetings, come talk to me or don't be surprised by your grade.

2. Two In-Class Quizzes (10%)

Short handwritten quizzes: a few identifications plus one paragraph, in the first 10-15 minutes of class. I will see if I can include brief ungraded warm-ups (on paper, start of class). I will count the higher of your two scores double and drop the lower half.

3. Blue-Book Midterm (25%) In-class, handwritten, Thursday, October 8 (before Fall Break). I will circulate the question pool in advance.

4. Literature Review (40%) The main writing assignment is a 2500-3000-word review of the literature on a GPE topic (excluding bibliography), expanding on a course topic or one of your own choosing, after consulting with me. Three parts: - Description & annotated bibliography (10%) — due Thursday, November 5 - Presentation, (10%) — week of November 17 - Final draft in narrative form (20%) — due Thursday, December 10 (finals period)

Course Structure

I will begin most Tuesday classes with an overview of the week's material. Thursdays are generally devoted to the discussion in groups. Students are expected to come to Thursday's class ready to talk having prepared discussion questions framed around the readings, alternating between two roles from week to week.

Connector — Ensures a continuous, semester-long conversation rather than a series of stand-alone reviews. Connect previous readings (and specific passages) to the current week's material, and construct a question that will stimulate discussion.

Applier — Applies the week's material to real-world events (contemporary or historical, reported in reputable outlets). Explain why and how the framework applies, in a way that provokes discussion and even disagreement.

Post your discussion question here every week, before Thursday's class: DWA 103 Discussion Questions doc — find the week's heading and add your name, role, and question (include your article link if you're the Applier). I pull from this doc to run Thursday's discussion.

About the course

Course Description

This is an undergraduate survey of the field of Global Political Economy (GPE). It is required for Diplomacy and World Affairs majors, and most enrolled students are DWA majors. It is intended for students who already have some background in International Relations and Economics and who want to explore the major theoretical, empirical, and policy perspectives on GPE.

The first part of the course covers the foundational theories of global political economy. The goal is to understand how theory is framed, how it "works," and what its potential inferences for practice might be. The second part explores globalization as a field of study with a focus on trade, finance, energy, and economic development.

During the second half of the semester, students also write a literature review project on a topic of their choice. Examples include: China–US rivalry; global kleptocracy; the green/circular economy; gender and the global economy; corporate citizenship and responsibility; the rise of populism/authoritarianism; the sustainable development goals (SDGs); social and economic rights; and more. This is an opportunity for you to explore a topic that is not covered in the regular course of the semester; or dive deeper into an aspect of something we did cover.

This course fulfills the Global Connections (CPGC) requirement of the college.

Course Objectives

This course covers the major theoretical traditions in global political economy (liberalism, realism/mercantilism, constructivism, and Marxism) and then applies them to trade, finance, energy, and development. It also trains the research and writing skills required by the literature review assignment.

Student Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, you will be able to:

  1. Identify and contrast the core claims of the four main GPE traditions, and say what evidence would count for or against each
  2. Summarize and evaluate a scholarly argument in the field (claims, evidence, and assumptions) in writing and out loud
  3. Locate, assess, and cite scholarly sources on a GPE topic
  4. Write a 2,500–3,000-word literature review that organizes a body of scholarship around a research puzzle in the conventions of the social sciences
  5. Apply a GPE framework to current or past events and defend the application

Course Workload

DWA 103 is a 4-unit course. On average, you should expect to spend at least twelve (12) hours a week (including in-class time) on this course.

Use of AI tools

Why this course is (mostly) an AI-free zone

I use some AI tools in research and teaching, but I also have had the advantage of being educated in the pre-AI era. I use them as an expert checking output against knowledge I already have. You are not yet experts. You are here to build that knowledge first. Your main goal in this course should be to do things that AI cannot do for you: build and defend your own causal arguments about politics and the world economy. Skills like reading closely, weighing evidence, constructing an argument, and being able to defend it in real time can develop only through practice. Experiencing that friction is the point of your education and growth. AI is genuinely useful for simulating expert workflows, but it takes away the "practice reps" that generate expertise in the first place. So this semester, we will be doing a lot more in-class testing, presentations and discussion without any aids. The take-home writing will be deliberately lower-stakes and structured in stages.

The rules

  1. In-class work (discussion, quizzes, the blue-book midterm, presentations): closed-book except where stated, and no AI — it's you, paper, and the room.
  2. Take-home writing: I expect it to be written without generative AI. I will not be able to verify this, which is exactly why these assignments are built in stages. You are welcome to use AI to learn, look for sources, verify data, generate illustrations to help your absorption of material, but you are always responsible for whatever winds up on the page with your name on it.
  3. No detectors. I will never run your work through an AI detector (they are unreliable and biased against non-native speakers and neurodivergent writers). If something in your work doesn't sound like you, we will have a conversation and I will ask you to walk me through your argument.
  4. When in doubt, ask first. A two-minute question before the fact is never an integrity problem.

Academic Integrity

Statement of the Shared Academic Integrity Commitment

Academic Integrity is a shared community value. It is built around trust and respect between members of the Occidental Community, and embodies a commitment to honesty and integrity in every aspect of one's academic life.

All members of the Occidental community are committed to uphold the highest degree of academic integrity. Unless stipulated otherwise, the academic work done for all assignments is expected to be the student's own; students are expected to give proper credit to the ideas and work of others.

Signing the Academic Integrity Commitment at the beginning of every semester represents a student's affirmation to uphold the shared values of honesty and integrity. When signing the Integrity Commitment associated with work in a course, students are affirming that they have not cheated, plagiarized, fabricated, or falsified information; nor assisted others in these actions.

Refer to Student Handbook for the framing of the commitment, definitions of Academic Ethics, and Process for alleged violations of the commitment.

And a word from me

People cut corners when they feel underprepared or out of time. I can help with both if you come early. I would much rather spend that 15 minutes with you than hours in misconduct hearings. If you're unsure whether something counts as a source or how to cite something properly, just ask.

Laptop & Smartphone Use

Laptops and smartphones may not be used during lectures (except with accommodations or prior approval). Please take notes on paper. Lots of research suggests that screens reduce attention spans even of people around you. You should have your laptop with you for in-class writing and discussion work. I will let you know when you can use it.

Mid-semester reports and incompletes

Mid-semester progress reports. Mid-semester progress reports are not intended to be used as determinative or predictive of the final grade in a course, but to provide an opportunity for the faculty to communicate an approximate estimation of progress by the students in the course by the midpoint of the current semester. I will submit yours during the Fall 2026 window (October 5–18) — after the midterm, so it reflects real information. Please look at it before you meet with your academic advisor.

Incompletes. You are eligible for an incomplete if you have completed at least one-third of the graded work, that work is of passing quality, and the Dean of Students Office has verified a personal circumstance that prevented you from finishing. Even then, the decision is mine. In this course an incomplete makes sense only when the only outstanding work is the final paper. If you're falling behind before the withdrawal deadline, come talk to me. Withdrawing is often the better outcome.
