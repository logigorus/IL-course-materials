Part I · The Economist 1998

“Exceptions that prove the rule”

ExceptionLogicCaveat in the article
Optimal tariffA large country cuts world demand, “tilting the terms of trade in their favour”
e.g., countries outside the WTO set higher tariffs on goods where they are big buyers (Broda, Limão & Weinstein 2008)
“rare for governments to be powerful enough”
e.g., US tariffs on China, 2018–19: import prices rose by about the full tariff, so US buyers paid (Amiti, Redding & Weinstein 2019)
Strategic trade policyIn scale industries, a tariff reserves the home market so a firm can “cut its costs and, with luck, … undercut foreign rivals” — “civil aircraft, semiconductors and cars”
e.g., Airbus, founded 1970 with state launch aid; out-delivers Boeing for the first time in 2003
“even rarer for them to have enough information”
e.g., Concorde (UK–France): 14 airliners in service, 1976–2003; development costs never recovered
Examples (instructor-composed): Broda, Limão & Weinstein, AER 98(5), 2008; Amiti, Redding & Weinstein, JEP 33(4), 2019; Airbus and Boeing annual delivery counts, 2003 (305 vs. 281); Concorde: British Airways 7, Air France 7
Part II · Rodrik 2012

Trade as technological progress: Henry Martyn, 1701

“We can produce textiles at home; or we can obtain the same quantity of textiles from India by producing another commodity which we sell in exchange. If the latter takes less labor than the former, it is the same as if a better technology for supplying textiles has dropped from the sky.”Rodrik 2012: 48, on Martyn’s Considerations upon the East-India Trade (1701)
  • Martyn’s examples: the sawmill (2 workers do the work of 30), the river barge (3 men move what 100 men and horses move on land)
  • “three quarters of a century before Adam Smith and more than a century before David Ricardo”

Part II · Rodrik 2012

The economist’s list of preconditions

1Liberalization “must be complete, covering all goods and trade partners” (a preferential agreement “is unlikely to satisfy the requirement”)
2“no microeconomic market imperfections other than the trade restrictions in question”
3The economy “must be ‘small’ in world markets,” or not on “the wrong side of the ‘optimum tariff’”
4“reasonably full employment,” or effective demand management
5Redistribution “should not be judged undesirable by society at large,” or compensating tax-transfer schemes exist
6No adverse fiscal effects, or “alternative and expedient ways of making up for the lost fiscal revenues”
7“politically sustainable and hence credible so that economic agents do not fear or anticipate a reversal”

“What Economists Will Not Tell You”

The reporter’s questionThe answer

Part II · Rodrik 2012

Two tests for opposition to trade

“A deserving argument against free trade must overcome at least one of the two hurdles mentioned above: the economic gains from freer trade must remain small compared to the distributional ‘costs’; and trade must entail practices that violate prevailing norms and social contracts at home.”Rodrik 2012: 61
  • Test 2 examples in the chapter: “employing child labor, repressing labor rights, or using environmentally harmful practices”
  • “Resistance to free trade is not just a matter of narrow self-interest or ignorance—at least not always.”

Part III · Milner 1999 · Institutions

Who decides: executives, legislatures, insulation

  • Reciprocal Trade Act of 1934: “giving the executive branch greater control over trade… made trade policy less susceptible to these influences and more free-trade oriented”
  • “In every successful reform effort, politicians delegated decisionmaking authority to units within the government that were insulated from routine bureaucratic processes, from legislative and interest group pressures, and even from executive pressure.” (Haggard & Webb 1994: 13)
  • Caveat: insulated policy makers “may actually be protectionists, in which case insulation allows greater protection” (Mansfield & Busch 1995)

Part III · Milner 1999 · Effects of trade

What openness does to politics

  • Liberalization “has a positive feedback effect on policy preferences and political strategies of domestic producer groups. As industries adjust… their characteristics change in ways that reduce the likelihood that they will demand protection in the future.” (Hathaway 1998: 606)
  • Open economies have large governments: compensation for “the losers from trade” (Cameron 1978; Katzenstein 1985)
  • “greater exposure to external risk, which trade promotes, increases the volatility of the domestic economy” (Rodrik 1997)
Part III · Milner 1999

Milner’s somewhat unsatisfying answer

“changing preferences among political leaders and societal groups, institutional changes (especially democratization), and the increased influence of international institutions that supported trade liberalization may best explain the global rush to free trade witnessed since 1980.… None of our existing theories by itself seems to do very well in explaining this movement… and none appears to have predicted it.”Milner 1999: 111

“Deep integration”
